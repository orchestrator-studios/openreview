Introduction
PTSD is due in part to decreased activation in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) and hippocampus (HPC), brain regions involved in fear extinction and recall of extinction learning (i.e., extinction retention). Consequently, a pharmacological agent that restores vmPFC-HPC function has the potential to reduce PTSD symptoms in the context of treatment that targets fear extinction.
Prolonged Exposure (PE) therapy, a first-line psychotherapy for PTSD [1], utilizes extinction learning to reduce emotional and physiological responses to trauma memories and reminders, thus reducing PTSD symptoms. Through repeatedly approaching the trauma memory and reminders in imaginal exposure (to the trauma memory) and in vivo exposure (to the people, places, and situations associated with the trauma), the trauma memory is modified or a new memory is generated that includes corrective information to reduce trauma-related fear and reactivity [2]. The beneficial effects of exposure therapy have been shown to derive from neurophysiological processes typical of fear extinction; that is, post-therapy increases in PFC activity in conjunction with decreases in amygdala (AMYG) activity [3]. Despite the effectiveness of PE, retention is a problem, with up to 50 % of patients not completing care, and some patients not fully responding [4,5]. PTSD remains a difficult-to-treat disorder, and medications that enhance the neural and neurochemical substrates of extinction, extinction recall, and extinction retention may augment PE response [6].
Evidence in rodent models of fear shows that activation of the cannabinoid (CB) system—specifically within the AMYG, vmPFC, and HPC—may regulate fear extinction and extinction recall. Drugs that block CB type 1 (CB1) receptors or genetic deletion of CB1 receptors within these structures prevent extinction recall, whereas activation of these same receptors, via agonists such as Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), promotes extinction recall [7,8]. Also, drugs that increase endogenous CB levels during fear extinction not only enhance extinction recall but also diminish the return of extinguished fear in rats (i.e., prevent spontaneous recovery of fear) [9].
In humans, pre-extinction administration of oral THC (7.5 mg dronabinol) has been shown to facilitate extinction recall and enhance vmPFC–hippocampal engagement during conditioned fear learning [10–12]. These findings support THC’s role in strengthening extinction-related neural plasticity and inform the present trial’s dosing and timing parameters. Earlier work [13] that used a higher dose (10 mg) and a simpler cue-based paradigm in healthy volunteers reported mixed results—likely due to differences in dose, timing, and task complexity. Our prior studies addressed these limitations by using lower, well-tolerated doses aligned with peak plasma levels and context-rich paradigms that yield robust neural and behavioral effects [14,15,16,17].
Administration of dronabinol (synthetic THC) prior to fear extinction facilitates subsequent extinction recall in healthy humans and in patients with PTSD, using a similar behavioral design as proposed in the current project [11,16]. Importantly, unlike many previous augmentation trials, which administered pharmacological agents without regard to their pharmacokinetics or alignment with the timing of therapeutic learning, our protocol is explicitly informed by findings from two prior studies that we conducted in adults with PTSD [16,17]. Those studies provided critical data on the optimal dose and timing of THC relative to extinction learning processes. As such, we designed this study to ensure that peak plasma levels of THC coincide with PE imaginal exposure sessions to maximize the drug’s potential to augment extinction-related neural plasticity. This first-in-human clinical trial therefore tests THC-assisted PE as a mechanistically grounded augmentation strategy, leveraging convergent evidence from our prior neuroimaging and psychophysiological research to link frontolimbic modulation to improved extinction retention.
This translationally informed, mechanism-driven approach enhances the likelihood of detecting true pharmacological augmentation effects and addresses a key limitation of earlier studies in this area. In the current protocol, we examine the ability of THC to increase the effectiveness of PE therapy for PTSD as evidenced in both a behavioral fear learning and extinction paradigm as well as reductions in PTSD severity.
Methods
Study design
We plan to recruit and consent up to 150 treatment-seeking adults with PTSD, ages 18–60. Based on previous massed PE studies, we anticipate some attrition due to loss of follow-up and withdrawal. Our goal is to randomize 60 of the 150 participants who complete the baseline assessments, pre-treatment fMRI, and early phase of treatment and are eligible to proceed to medication administration. Consistent with covariate-adaptive randomization procedures, randomization will occur following completion of PE sessions 1 and 2 (psychoeducation) and immediately prior to Session 3, when participants receive study medication (7.5 mg THC) or placebo (PBO), [18]. This timing minimizes the risk of randomizing participants who later withdraw before any medicated exposure sessions, thereby preserving randomization balance.
We assess PTSD symptom severity at each visit and at 3-month follow-up. In fMRI, patients complete a fear conditioning paradigm to measure brain activation during fear extinction at pre and posttreatment. Because the PE protocol is delivered in a “massed” format—up to three sessions per week—this design allows extinction learning to occur over a compressed timescale; prior evidence indicates that massed PE produces comparable outcomes to standard weekly delivery [19,20]. The study received approval from the Wayne State University Institutional Review Board and is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04080427).
Consented participants complete the following procedures (Fig. 1). Following baseline assessments and prior to beginning PE therapy, participants complete a fear conditioning paradigm (Fig. 2) including fear acquisition, extinction learning, extinction recall and renewal during fMRI scanning. At posttreatment, they complete assessments and another fear conditioning paradigm during fMRI scanning (except with the use of novel conditioned stimuli [CSs], Fig. 2). Major assessments occur at pretreatment, session 6, posttreatment and 3-month follow-up. Self-reports are collected in sessions 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, and 10 (see Table 2).
Power analysis
Previous studies of other pharmacological agents (e.g., D-cyclo-serine) to augment exposure therapy in patients with anxiety disorders and PTSD have shown that samples of 10 to 23 participants in each condition (active vs. PBO) provides adequate power to observe condition differences in symptoms post-treatment and several months later [21], as well as changes in vmPFC activation to feared stimuli [22]). Effect sizes in previous studies ranged from moderate to large (Cohen d = 0.43–1.18); thus, the anticipated cohort of 30 completers per condition (PTSD-THC, PTSD-PBO) yields at least 80 % power to detect moderate to large effects. However, given that no prior clinical trial has evaluated THC as an adjunct to exposure therapy, this study is considered exploratory and intended primarily to estimate effect sizes and feasibility parameters for future confirmatory R01-scale trials rather than to test definitive efficacy.
Recruitment and screening
Participants are recruited from the Detroit community using flyers, mass e-mails, and webpage advertisements; and from health/mental health facilities: Wayne State University (WSU) Physicians Group, North Central Health Center, St. John Providence Eastwood Clinics, WSU Psychology Clinic, and WSU Clinical Research Center. Interested individuals complete a confidential pre-screening survey online (via Qualtrics) or over the phone with research staff. If pre-screen eligible, they are scheduled for the in-person pretreatment assessment, screening, and orientation session, where they complete informed consent if eligible and interested. This recruitment strategy mirrors those used in our prior PTSD studies and has yielded high retention even with multiple assessment points, suggesting that participant burden from study procedures is manageable. Randomization and masking
Random assignment to conditions occurs after Session 2 and before Session 3 using covariate adaptive randomization [23,24,25,26,27], in which each new participant is sequentially allocated to a treatment condition by taking into account baseline characteristics (e.g., CAPS-5, PCL-5, age, sex, race) identified a priori during study design, prior to any participant enrollment, based on theoretical and empirical relevance to study outcomes, and previous assignments of participants [28]. Consistent with CONSORT guidelines and best practices in the reporting of randomized trials, no statistical tests of baseline differences between groups will be conducted.
Random assignment is to one of two conditions: 7.5 mg THC (dronabinol; optimal dose selected based on previous study [17]) or PBO. Dronabinol is administered orally and is placed in opaque capsules with dextrose filler. PBO capsules are identical in appearance but contain only dextrose. All capsules are double-blind administered at four exposure sessions (PE Sessions 3–6) with 1 capsule per session. This approach was selected to pair medication administration specifically with the first four imaginal exposure sessions—the phase when extinction learning and consolidation are most active—while limiting total drug exposure to optimize safety in this first-in-human augmentation trial. Dronabinol or PBO is dispensed by the PI, who possesses a Schedule II-V controlled substance research license through the State of Michigan and the DEA and has experience in the use of dronabinol and PBO in human research. A registered pharmacist over-encapsulates the required dronabinol and creates the matching PBO.
Interventions
THC and PBO
At each of the first four imaginal exposure sessions (Sessions 3–6), a capsule (dronabinol or PBO) is ingested just before the session. Based on prior pharmacokinetic and neuroimaging work demonstrating that oral THC reaches peak plasma concentration and subjective effect approximately 120 min post-dose [29,11,30], PE is initiated ≈ 120 min after ingestion to synchronize peak THC levels with extinction learning [29,11,30]. This alignment is intended to maximize CB1-mediated extinction processes while maintaining low subjective intoxication, as demonstrated in our prior human studies [10–12,31]. Because oral THC has a long half-life [29] plasma samples are collected after THC administration prior to, during, and after each PE session to monitor THC levels. Participants and staff remain blinded to condition, and all procedures are performed under PI supervision to ensure safety and regulatory compliance (DEA Schedule II-V license on file).
PE treatment, provider training and fidelity
PE is conducted per the treatment manual [2] in 10, 90-min sessions in a “massed” format; that is, up to 3 sessions per week (rather than once per week). Evidence from recent RCTs demonstrates that massed PE is non-inferior to standard weekly delivery and well tolerated in military and civilian samples [19,20]. Sessions 1 and 2 focus on psychoeducation (reactions to trauma, treatment rationale, breathing retraining, etc.). Sessions 3–10 include imaginal exposure to the trauma memory and in vivo exposure to trauma related avoided people, places, and situations. Patients practice exposures (e.g., listen to tapes of imaginal exposure, carry out in vivo exposure) between sessions.
All PE providers are postdoctoral psychology fellows at Emory University School of Medicine who have completed PE certification and consultation with Dr. Rauch. Therapy is conducted remotely using Emory University’s HIPAA-compliant telehealth. To ensure therapist fidelity and adherence to protocol, all PE therapy sessions are video recorded, and 20 % are rated for fidelity. A licensed psychologist (M.L.) in Detroit provides local emergency clinical coverage.
Measures
Fear conditioning paradigm with fMRI and skin conductance
This fMRI protocol uses an event-related design developed and validated in Dr. Rabinak’s lab [31] (Fig. 2). MR scanning at WSU is performed on a 3.0 Tesla Siemens Magnetom Verio System using an industry-leading 32-channel head-coil for superior image quality. Whole-brain fMRI blood oxygen-level dependent (BOLD)-related signal measures are acquired to measure task- and drug-related effects and to minimize susceptibility artifact (signal loss) at the medial temporal lobe (including the AMYG, HPC, and the vmPFC) [32].
The key comparison of this task, contrasting CS + E and CS + U during extinction recall in Context B (see Fig. 2) allows us to assess physiological and brain responses specific to recall of extinction learning and independent from recall of fear acquisition. Contrasting CS + E and CS + A will examine real-time avoidant behavior impact on fear responding during recall of extinction learning. During extinction learning, the CS + A stimulus is presented with an active-avoidance option—participants can press a designated button to prevent the virtual bag from opening—allowing us to quantify avoidance behavior within the extinction context (see Fig. 2). To test for contextual fear renewal (context shift from the extinction context [B] to the conditioning context [A]), we conduct a proximal fear renewal test immediately after the fear extinction recall test [presented with the CS + E, CS + A, and CS + U (all with no snake) in Context A].
All phases of the paradigm are conducted in the MRI while recording SCR and US expectancy ratings. During all sessions, our primary measures of conditioned fear is SCR magnitude and US expectancy ratings for each CS trial [33]. Participants complete the entire fear conditioning paradigm (i.e., fear acquisition, extinction, recall and renewal) two times (pre-PE treatment and post-PE). For the post-treatment, novel contexts (e.g., Context C, ‘garden’ and Context D, ‘lobby’) and stimuli (e. g., black, white, and gray messenger bags) are used.
Assessment of fear learning is operationalized as a change in Skin Conductance Response (SCR) magnitude. The SCR signal is amplified and continuously sampled at 1000 Hz, and AcqKnowledge software is used for analysis (BIOPAC Systems). In the current study, SCR data will be processed using a recently developed, data-driven Bayesian linear-modeling approach from our group, which provides more robust and reliable estimation of stimulus-evoked responses than traditional peak-to-trough scoring methods. This updated method minimizes noise and improves cross-study comparability, addressing the inconsistency of earlier analytic pipelines that limited SCR inclusion in some of our prior THC studies [11,12,17]. Extinction recall is assessed by comparing the mean differential response during CS + E vs. CS + U. The inclusion of SCR alongside fMRI BOLD activation provides a multi-level index of extinction retention, permitting validation of neural mechanisms against peripheral physiological markers.
Genotyping
For patients who consent to DNA collection, extraction and genotyping of the rs324420 variant is conducted using Qiagen buccal DNA kits (Germantown, MD). This variant within the FAAH gene has been linked to reduced FAAH enzymatic activity and elevated endocannabinoid signaling, which may influence individual variability in extinction learning and drug response [34,35].
THC concentration
Plasma concentrations of 7.5 mg THC, 11-OH-THC (THC’s active metabolite), and THC-COOH (THC’s inactive metabolite) are quantified from blood samples drawn via venipuncture of 3 mL at 3 time points during PE Sessions 3–6: 1) before receiving THC or PBO; 2) approximately 120 min [2 h] after receiving THC or PBO; 3) at the end of the study session (approximately 210 min [3.5 h] after receiving THC or PBO). Plasma concentrations of the primary endocannabinoids (2-AG, anandamide) are quantified in these samples. Concentration levels are determined using ultra performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC/MS/MS) [36]. Based on previous studies we expect peak plasma THC concentration will occur ≈120 min post-administration [11,30]. This multi-timepoint design allows evaluation of pharmacokinetic variability and exploration of associations between plasma cannabinoid levels, neural activation, and extinction performance. These analyses will inform future dose-optimization studies and clarify the pharmacodynamic window most relevant for extinction learning.
Psychological measures
Several symptom and distress measures are completed at intake to determine eligibility, and some measures are repeated during the study to assess change (Table 2). All interview assessments are conducted by trained assessors who are blind to study condition, and 40 % of assessments are rated for interrater reliability.
Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview Version 7.0.2 (MINI [37] is a structured diagnostic interview for major psychiatric diagnoses and is used to determine eligibility and assess for co-morbid diagnoses at intake.
Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5 (CAPS-5[38] is a gold- standard clinician interview assessing PTSD diagnosis and severity. The past-week version is used during the treatment period. The past-month version is used at major assessments.
Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS; Posner et al., [39]) is a standardized 8 point clinician-administered assessment of past suicidal ideation and behaviors and is used to determine eligibility at intake (lifetime version) and to assess suicidal ideation and behaviors since last contact at PE sessions 3–10 and major assessments.
PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5[40] is a 20-item self-report assessment of PTSD severity used for screening and monitoring symptom change.
Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI [41] is a self-rated questionnaire assessing sleep disturbance and quality over the past month.
Subjective Units of Distress Scale (0 = absolutely calm; 100 = worst distress) assesses subjective negative affect and is created collaboratively with the PE provider [2]. SUDS are collected during therapy sessions involving imaginal exposure and during the fear conditioning paradigm to collect stimulus-specific ratings of anxiety.
Spielberger State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI)[42] (state subscale) assesses current anxiety and is collected at intake, in PE sessions 3–10 and at 3- and 6-month follow-up.
Together, these measures provide both clinician-rated and selfreported indices of symptom change, emotional distress, and functional improvement across the treatment trajectory.
THC/PBO effect measures
Several measures assessing the effects of THC/PBO ingestion are collected at sessions 3–6 before capsule ingestion, approximately 120 min after ingestion, and at the end of the session.
Drug Effects Questionnaire (DEQ) [43] assesses the extent to which participants “feel any substance effect(s),” “feel high,” “like the effects,” “dislike the effects,” and “want more of the substance” using 100 mm visual analog scales (VAS) anchored at “not at all” and “extremely.” These questionnaires are sensitive to the effects of a variety of psychoactive drugs on affective state/mood and drug liking [44,45].
Current Mood. We use 100-mm VAS to obtain ratings on a number of mood/symptom descriptors (e.g., clear- headed, anxious, jittery) anchored at “not at all” and “extremely.” Participants are instructed to place a mark on each line indicating how they feel at the moment.
Addiction Research Center Inventory (ARCI) [46,47] is a 53-item true-false questionnaire with five empirically-derived scales that are sensitive to the effects of a variety of classes of abused drugs.
End of Session Questionnaire (ESQ) asks participants the extent to which they think they felt a drug effect, the extent to which they think they received THC or PBO, and how much they “liked its effects.” Participants are asked to comment on any unusual effects they experienced, and whether they would take the substance again. Responses from the ESQ are used to assess blinding efficacy for both participants and study staff (as parallel staff ratings are also collected post-session).
Treatment completion, response, drop and missing data
Treatment completion is defined as completion of at least 4 exposurebased sessions of PE with pill ingestion. Response is defined as at least 50 % reduction in CAPS-5 total, and remission is defined as loss of PTSD diagnosis plus a score of <20 on total CAPS-5 [48]. These operational definitions align with established clinical trial benchmarks and facilitate comparability with other exposure-based treatment augmentation studies.
Data analytic plan
All analyses will follow an intent-to-treat (ITT) framework, with sensitivity analyses using as-treated and per-protocol approaches as appropriate. The intention-to-treat (ITT) sample will include all participants who are randomized (n =60), regardless of treatment adherence or study completion. Participants who are consented but not randomized will not be included in the ITT analyses, but their characteristics and reasons for attrition will be summarized to evaluate generalizability and potential bias. Statistical significance will be evaluated using two-tailed tests with an alpha level of 0.05. Effect sizes and 90 % confidence intervals will be reported. Multiple comparisons within hypothesis families will be corrected using the Hochberg step-down procedure (Hochberg, [49]). Because this is a first-in-human, mechanistic pilot trial, analyses emphasize estimation of effect magnitudes and credible patterns of change rather than strict null-hypothesis testing. When appropriate, Bayesian mixed-model approaches will complement frequentist analyses to quantify evidence strength and uncertainty around observed effects.
General analytic strategy
Longitudinal clinical and behavioral data will be analyzed using linear and generalized linear mixed-effects models (LMM/GLMM) to account for intra-subject correlation, therapist clustering, unbalanced designs, and missingness. For continuous outcomes, LMMs will model random intercepts and slopes as appropriate. Primary comparison will be Condition x Time. For binary outcomes (e.g., remission, treatment dropout), GLMMs with logit link will be used. Analyses will include relevant covariates (e.g., baseline symptom severity, age, sex, and therapist) identified a priori during study design to improve precision and adjust for residual confounding.
fMRI analysis
Functional neuroimaging data will be preprocessed and analyzed using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12; Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging). Analyses will include two complementary approaches: (1) Region of Interest (ROI) Analysis: Hypothesis-driven ROIs will include the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), HPC, and AMYG, defined using anatomical atlases (e.g.,[50,51]. Additional spherical ROIs (5-mm radius) centered on peak activation coordinates identified in prior work [16,31] will be used to assess replication. Extracted BOLD signal will be submitted to repeated measures MANCOVA with Drug (THC, placebo) as the between-subjects factor, and Stimulus (CS +E, CS +U) and Time (pre-, post-treatment) as within-subject factors. Covariates will be included as necessary. Significant MANCOVAs will be followed by ANCOVAs and simple-effects tests. (2) Whole-Brain Voxel-Wise Analysis: Exploratory whole-brain analyses will use voxel-wise approaches with appropriate corrections. (3) Exploratory Connectivity Analysis: Task-dependent functional connectivity will be assessed using generalized psychophysiological interaction (gPPI) analyses (Friston et al., [52]; Banks et al., [53]). Separate connectivity models will be run using vmPFC, AMYG, and HPC as seed regions in each treatment condition (THC, placebo), followed by between-condition comparisons during extinction recall and fear renewal. Exploratory Bayesian ROI analyses will also be performed to estimate posterior effect sizes for key regions, providing complementary inference on the strength of THC effects on extinction-related activation.
SCR analysis
Skin conductance data will be analyzed using a Bayesian hierarchical linear modeling approach at the trial level, replacing traditional peak-to-trough scoring methods. This data-driven method provides more robust and reliable estimation of stimulus-evoked responses and accommodates trial-level variability and missing data [54]. Continuous electrodermal activity is recorded at 1000 Hz, and amplitude SCR (aSCR) is derived as the maximum deflection 1–5 s after CS onset. Values are square-root transformed and z-scored within participant. Artifacted trials are excluded for SCR only, and low responders (< 10 % valid responses ≥0.02 μS) are retained under partial pooling.
Trial-level standardized aSCR values are modeled as a function of Condition (CS + E, CS + U, CS + A), Phase (Acquisition, Extinction, Recall, Renewal), Timepoint (Pre-, Post-treatment), Group (THC vs placebo), and Trial (linear effect, z-scored), with interactions necessary to test extinction-recall and avoidance contrasts. Random effects include by-participant intercepts and slopes for within-subject factors and bystimulus intercepts (bag identity). Models use a Student-t likelihood and weakly informative priors (Normal[0,1] for fixed effects; Student-t [3,0,1] for SDs; LKJ[2] for correlations). Estimation is conducted in Stan via brms (4 chains, 4000 iterations, R^<1.01\hat R < 1.01R^<1.01).
Key contrasts include (1) CS+E vs CS+U during extinction recall (extinction retention) and (2) CS+E vs CS+A to examine avoidance modulation. Posterior medians, 95 % credible intervals, and posterior probabilities [Pr(β > 0)] will be reported alongside region-of-practical-equivalence (ROPE) indices (±0.1 SD). Sensitivity analyses will compare Gaussian vs Student-t likelihoods, exclude non-responders, test alternative transformations, include trial-wise US-expectancy covariates, and add plasma THC concentration (z-scored) to evaluate pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic coupling.
Planned analyses by aim
Aim 1 (Primary Functional Outcomes):
Examine the effect of THC administered before each PE session on symptom severity in PTSD patients during and after PE treatment. Hypothesis 1 A: Relative to PBO, patients in the THC condition will exhibit more improvement (greater reduction in symptom severity based on CAPS-5 and/or PCL-5) over the course of PE treatment. To evaluate symptom reduction across prolonged exposure (PE) sessions, piecewise linear mixed-effects models will estimate separate slopes for distinct treatment phases (baseline → Session 6, Session 6 → Session 10, and Session 10 → follow-up), allowing the early medicated phase (Sessions 3–6) to be modeled independently. Condition (THC vs. placebo) and condition-by-time interactions will test Hypothesis 1 A. PTSD symptoms (CAPS-5, PCL-5) will serve as primary outcomes. Remission and responder status will be evaluated using GLMMs with logit link, adjusting for therapist clustering. Given the pilot nature of the study, analyses will emphasize effect-size estimation and credible trends rather than binary significance thresholds.
Aim 2 (External Validity):
Determine whether brain activation and/or peripheral responses (SCRs, US expectancy) during recall of extinction learning are altered following THC-assisted PE treatment in PTSD patients. Hypothesis 2 A: Relative to PBO, THC will increase vmPFC and/or HPC activation to the CS+E during recall of extinction learning pre- vs. post-treatment. For Hypothesis 2 A, BOLD activation to CS+E versus CS+U will be compared pre- and post-treatment across ROIs using MANCOVA. Hypothesis 2B: Relative to PBO, THC will decrease peripheral responses (e.g., SCRs; US expectancy ratings, SUDS) to the CS+E during recall of extinction learning pre- vs. post-treatment. Hypothesis 2B will examine SCRs, US-expectancy ratings, and SUDS using Bayesian hierarchical linear modeling for trial-level SCR data and linear mixed-effects models for other continuous outcomes. This approach accounts for repeated measures, missingness, and individual variability while estimating posterior effect sizes and probabilities of direction. Planned contrasts will compare CS+E vs CS+U (extinction recall) and CS+E vs CS+A (avoidance), with credible intervals and posterior predictive checks used to interpret results. Correlational analyses will explore associations between change in vmPFC/HPC activation and reductions in physiological and clinical fear responses, testing the translational link between neural and behavioral extinction indices.
Exploratory Aim 3:
Examine the effect of THC administered before each PE session on response and remission. Hypothesis 3 A: Relative to PBO, more patients in the THC condition will achieve response and/or remission. Hypothesis 3 A will evaluate condition differences in response/remission, dropout, and treatment adherence using GLMMs. As-treated (per protocol) analyses will censor outcomes at discontinuation and provide complementary estimates of treatment effects. These will be compared to ITT findings to assess consistency. Effect sizes (odds ratios and confidence intervals) will be emphasized to inform design parameters for future adequately powered R01-scale trials.
Exploratory Aim 4:
Examine the effect of THC administered before each PE session on fear reactivity. Hypothesis 4 A: Relative to PBO, patients in the THC condition will report greater reduction in fear reactivity to trauma-related cues during PE treatment. [28]. Hypothesis 4 A will examine whether THC facilitates extinction learning as reflected in reduced fear reactivity (SUDS) across sessions. A within-subject extinction index will be calculated from peak SUDS differences across successive sessions. This metric will be entered as a fixed effect in mixed-effects models predicting post-treatment symptom scores, with Drug and Extinction Index × Drug interaction terms. Therapist will be modeled as a random effect. Models will adjust for baseline symptom levels and other relevant covariates. Secondary outcomes (e.g., anxiety, depression) will be analyzed using similar models. Parallel exploratory Bayesian models will be used to quantify the probability that THC produces clinically meaningful reductions in fear reactivity, providing an interpretable measure of evidence strength.
All psychophysiological analyses of trial-level skin conductance data (aSCR) described under Hypothesis 2B and related exploratory aims will follow the Bayesian hierarchical modeling framework detailed in Section 2.7.2, ensuring consistency across primary and secondary analyses.
Discussion
The primary objective of this randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled augmentation clinical trial is to use the specific parameters developed from previous translational neuroscience studies in a clinical PTSD treatment seeking sample to examine whether THC can augment the benefits of PE. Specifically, we are testing whether 7.5 mg of synthetic THC in the form of dronabinol can augment: 1) extinction retention, and/or 2) PTSD symptom reduction in the context of massed PE for PTSD. Although PE is effective for PTSD, retention and partial response remain issues of concern with only about 50 % of those receiving PE in clinical care completing an effective episode of care [4]. Massed PE, which delivers multiple sessions per week, has been shown to increase retention with similar outcomes [20,55]. Thus, maximizing power for the mechanistic comparisons of enhanced extinction learning and facilitation of consolidation of therapeutic learning.
The study’s importance includes its potential impact on increasing or accelerating response to PE, which could enhance remission rates and potentially increase retention. In contrast to many previous augmentation trials, the current protocol builds directly on prior animal and human conditioning work of the PI (CR) and colleagues, as well as the PE expertise of co-I (SR) [15,10,11,56,17]. Specifically, the dose (7.5 mg) and timing (≈120 min pre-exposure) were selected based on empirical evidence showing that this regimen optimally engages cannabinoid receptors during extinction learning without impairing performance or producing adverse subjective effects. This approach is consistent with prior mechanistic studies demonstrating that acute THC administration enhances extinction retention and vmPFC activation [13,11,17].
The use of targeted, session-specific dosing with THC, compared to continuous or daily administration, may reduce the potential side effects and the risk for misuse or tolerance development in those who receive the intervention. In addition, this strategy aligns with neurobiological principles—cannabinoid receptor availability must be preserved between dosing sessions to allow THC binding at extinction-relevant time points. If effective, this augmentation strategy is already available for prescription and could be implemented in clinical care relatively quickly, with minimal barriers beyond prescriber oversight.
In addition to providing direction on the clinical augmentation potential of dronabinol, the study also explores the role of extinction learning and retention in response to PE in PTSD. Specifically, the study design allows for examination of whether individuals with specific baseline characteristics of extinction learning (i.e., lower extinction retention) are less likely to respond to PE, and whether this relationship differs between dronabinol or placebo. Since the fear learning and extinction paradigm is conducted in the fMRI scanner, the exploration includes both neural activation patterns (vmPFC, hippocampus, amygdala) and peripheral indices (skin conductance, US expectancy). This multimodal approach may provide a deeper understanding of the neural and physiological mechanisms that predict better treatment response. While this increases patient burden to complete mechanistic and symptom assessments, both are required to ensure understanding of the therapeutic outcomes and previous translational treatment outcome studies in our lab and others have been successful in simultaneously monitoring these processes [57].
Finally, repeating the fear learning and extinction paradigm after treatment can provide insight into whether PE impacts extinction learning processes and whether that impact differs between dronabinol and placebo. Thus, the design examines both what (symptom and extinction retention change) and the how (genetic, autonomic, and neural mechanisms) underlying PE response with THC augmentation. By integrating mechanistic neuroscience with a scalable psychotherapeutic intervention, this work may help refine individualized treatment approaches and identify biomarkers predictive of exposure therapy success.
