Introduction
In the most recent survey of illicit drug use in the United States, marijuana prevailed as the most commonly used drug in the country (NSDUH, 2018). Changes to marijuana laws across the country have also diminished the plant’s illicit status over the past few years. Marijuana is currently legal for medicinal purposes in 33 states and Washington, D.C., while 11 states and Washington D.C. have legalized it for recreational use (DISA, 2020). Drug companies have also developed three FDA-approved synthetic formulations: two containing dronabinol (Marinol and Syndros) and one containing nabilone (Cesamet) (Abuhasira et al., 2018). Although these formulations are only approved to treat nausea and vomiting associated with chemotherapy, and anorexia associated with AIDS, their use could be easily expanded to the many other potential therapeutic uses for marijuana, including chronic pain, neuropathic pain and spasticity, and muscle stiffness associated with multiple sclerosis (Hill, 2015).
As the use of marijuana continues to expand, previous concerns surrounding its major psychoactive cannabinoid, Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ9-THC), have been marginalized. In addition to having therapeutic potential, Δ9-THC has also been found to cause impairments to memory by disrupting the storage and retrieval of newly acquired information. For example, Δ9-THC disrupts the recall of word lists and prose material learned under the influence of the drug (Abel, 1971b; Darley et al., 1973; Miller et al., 1977; Ranganathan et al., 2017). Similar effects have been shown preclinically in rodents and non-human primates utilizing behavioral tasks such as passive avoidance, the Morris water maze, radial-arm maze, and operant procedures for studying learning and performance (Lichtman et al., 1995; Lichtman and Martin, 1996; Mishima et al., 2001; Nakamura et al., 1991; Niyuhire et al., 2007; Winsauer et al., 1999).
Although Δ9-THC has affinity for both cannabinoid type-1 (CB1) and type-2 (CB2) receptors, its central nervous system effects, including its disruptive effects on memory, are mediated by CB1 receptors because they are the predominant subtype located in the brain (Mechoulam and Parker, 2013). These CB1 selective effects on memory have been confirmed using the CB1 selective antagonist, rimonanbant, which has been shown to block the memory-impairing effects of Δ9-THC. In rats trained on a conditional discrimination and non-match-to-position task, rimonabant attenuated the Δ9-THC-induced decrease in choice accuracy (Mallet and Beninger, 1998). Similarly, in mice trained on a Morris water-maze procedure, rimonabant blocked the Δ9-THC-induced increase in latency to find the submerged platform (Da and Takahashi, 2002). In addition, intrahippocampal administration of rimonabant was found to block the disruptive effects of Δ9-THC on an eight-arm radial-maze task in rats, indicating that the memory-impairing effects of Δ9-THC are due to CB1 receptors in the hippocampus (Wise et al., 2009).
Well known for its importance in memory processes, the hippocampus is densely populated with CB1 receptors (Hampson and Deadwyler, 1998). Lesions to the hippocampus have been found to produce memory-impairing effects analogous to those produced by Δ9-THC administration. For example, rats administered Δ9-THC showed a similar decrease in delayed matching-to-sample behavior as rats with hippocampal lesions (Hampson et al., 1999; Heyser et al., 1993). Likewise, in humans, subjects receiving an inhaled dose of marijuana showed deficits comparable to subjects with hippocampal damage in a story recall task (Drew et al., 1980). These similarities further suggest that activation of CB1 receptors by Δ9–THC in the hippocampus is responsible for the drug’s disruptive effects on memory.
Another concern over the increased use of the cannabinoids is the strong possibility that they may produce different effects in men and women, and that these sex-dependent effects may be mediated by the sex hormones. This possibility is also relevant to the potentially adverse CB1-mediated effects of the cannabinoids because estrogen plays an important role in cognitive function, specifically hippocampally-sensitive tasks (Korol and Pisani, 2015). This may be due to the hormone’s effect on dendritic spine density in the hippocampus, which has been closely linked to learning and memory. Beltran-Campos et al. (2011) found that female rats that had undergone training in the Morris water-maze had an increased spine density compared to swimming controls, and that this increase was reversed by ovariectomy. This suggests a link between dendritic spine density and learning and memory, and may also explain how estrogen is able to enhance learning and memory (Sandstrom and Williams, 2001). Within-subject behavioral data from our lab has shown that implantation of 17β-estradiol versus vehicle capsules in ovariectomized (OVX) female rats decreased percent errors during the acquisition of a sequence of nose pokes in a two-component operant procedure: one component in which a new sequence had to be acquired each session and one component in which a well-rehearsed (known) sequence had to be emitted (Daniel et al., 2002). Moreover, 17β-estradiol was able to decrease learning errors without altering response rate, which demonstrated the selectivity of these estrogen-related effects for learning, as opposed to motor, mechanisms. 17β-estradiol administration also attenuated both the error-increasing and rate-decreasing effects produced by acute Δ9-THC administration in both components of the procedure, suggesting that the interaction between estrogen and the cannabinoids could extend beyond learning mechanisms.
With the aforementioned results indicating there are clear CB1-mediated disruptions of learning produced by Δ9-THC, and that the sex hormone 17β-estradiol can attenuate these effects, we were interested in examining the interaction between Δ9-THC and 17β-estradiol on the retention and retrieval of information acquired before administration of the drug. In other words, can Δ9-THC disrupt the recall of information after the information has already been learned and consolidated into memory, and could these disruptions be reduced by 17β-estradiol administration? In order to answer this question a complex behavioral task, repeated acquisition and delayed-performance, was used to specifically assess the retention of newly acquired information. This procedure required subjects to learn a sequence of responses, retain that sequential information over a delay, and then emit that sequence again. One advantage of this procedure is that retention of the information can be examined over lengthy delays that allow for the administration of the drug after sequence acquisition so that the effects of drug on memory can be assessed independently of a potential disruption of learning.
Methods
Subjects:
Thirty-eight female Long-Evans hooded rats (Envigo, Indianapolis, IN) maintained at 90% of their free-feeding weight were used as part of a within-subject behavioral design and a between-group biochemical design to investigate the effects of Δ9–THC on memory before and after two hormone manipulations. The first within-subject hormone manipulation was to chronically administer 17β-estradiol to ovariectomized adult females. The second within-subject hormone manipulation was to chronically administer the nonselective estrogen receptor modifier tamoxifen to intact adult females. As depicted in Figure 1, assessing the hormonal and morphological effects of those manipulations also required some OVX (n=6) and intact (n=6) subjects to be sacrificed prior to these manipulations.
During the experimental sessions, all subjects earned 45-mg food pellets and were fed chow post-session, if necessary to maintain a consistent weight. Water was available ad libitum in the home cage. Subjects were housed in individual cages and kept in a colony room maintained at 21±2°C and 50±10% relative humidity on a 14-h light/10-h dark cycle beginning at 6 a.m. Animals were handled in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Louisiana State University Health Sciences Center in New Orleans and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council, 2011)
Apparatus:
Sixteen identical operant chambers from Coulbourn Instruments (Harvard Apparatus, Inc., Holliston, MA) were used for all behavioral training and testing. The front wall of each chamber was equipped with a house light, speaker, feeding trough with a light, and three response keys aligned horizontally (8 cm apart, center to center, and 14.5 cm above the floor). Each of the response keys could be transilluminated by green light-emitting diodes (LEDs) in 3 different dice-like patterns; however, these three patterns could be illuminated in different combinations to produce a total of seven different patterns. In the present study, four of these patterns were used as discriminative stimuli in the behavioral procedure. Located behind the front wall of the chamber were a 28-volt mechanical relay that provided an audible click upon activation and a pellet dispenser for 45-mg food pellets (LabTab AIN-76A, TestDiet, Richmond, IN). Each chamber was enclosed within a sound-attenuating cubicle with double doors that closed magnetically. The speaker provided white noise to mask extraneous sounds and a fan located inside the cubicle provided ventilation. Med-PC IV software for Windows (Med Associates, Inc., Fairfax, VT) was used for programming and executing the program. Each chamber was also connected to a cumulative recorder (Gerbrands Corporation, Arlington, MA) located in the same room.
Procedure:
As shown in panel A of Figure 2, three phases comprised the repeated acquisition and delayed-performance procedure: acquisition, delay, and delayed-performance. More specifically, the subject’s task each session was to acquire a four-response sequence to a criterion, retain that information over a delay, and then emit that same sequence during delayed-performance. To train this complex task, however, required incremental stepwise training as described below.
Acquisition Training
Preliminary training began at approximately 60 days of age with acclimation to the operant chamber, magazine training, shaping of the nose-poke response, and reinforcing responses on a single key when all three response keys (right, left, and center) were illuminated with the stimulus that would ultimately end the sequence (i.e., four green dots). A “correct” response in this one-response task produced an audible click of the mechanical relay, extinguished the stimuli, illuminated the feeder trough for 0.4 seconds, delivered a food pellet, and then re-illuminated all three response keys after pellet delivery. When subjects responded on an incorrect key (e.g., the left [L] or right [R] key when the center [C] key was correct), the error was followed by a 5-sec period in which the key lights were extinguished and responses had no programmed consequences (i.e., timeout). After rats reliably responded (>100 responses) under this fixed-ratio (FR) one schedule for food presentation, acquisition of a two-response sequence was initiated.
During this training period, each of the three keys was illuminated with six green dots and the correct response transitioned the stimulus to four green dots and the next correct sequence position (e.g., LR, LC, CR, CL, RL and RC). Making a second correct response then resulted in reinforcement under what is termed a second-order FR-1 schedule (FR 1[chain FR 2(FR 1):S]) where the two-response sequence only needed to be completed one time for reinforcement. As above, errors in either sequence position produced a 5-second timeout, followed by the illumination of the pattern that was illuminated prior to the timeout; that is, errors did not reset the sequence (see right-hand portion of Figure 2B). Only a correct response resulted in a stimulus change, which was dependent upon the subject’s current position in the sequence.
When stable acquisition of the two-response sequences was evident from visual inspection of the data, a third and fourth response with respective stimuli were added to the sequence. In addition, the second-order FR schedule was gradually increased from a second-order FR 1 to a FR 3 (i.e., FR 3[chain FR 4(FR 1):S]), which meant the four-response sequence had to be completed three times for reinforcement. As in all of the other stages of acquisition training, errors emitted during the acquisition of the four-response sequences produced a 5-sec timeout, but did not reset the sequence.
Repeated Acquisition of Response Sequences
In general, the acquisition phase of this procedure is termed repeated acquisition because the four-response sequence in the acquisition phase was changed from session to session (Thompson, 1973). As illustrated in Figure 2B, reinforcement was dependent upon the subject emitting the correct response in the presence of each of the four different stimuli, which consisted of (1) a single green dot for the first response, (2) five green dots (cross) for the second response, (3) six green dots (full array) for the third response and (4) four green dots (diamond) for the fourth response. This type of sequential responding is procedurally defined as a “chain” because each response except the last produces a discriminative stimulus controlling the response that follows (Kelleher, 1966).
Examples of sequences for five consecutive sessions were: Right-Left-Right-Center (as shown in Figure 2B) or CLRC, LRCR, RCLC, CRCL, and RLRC. In fact, all of the four-response sequences were carefully selected to be equivalent in several ways and there were restrictions on their ordering across sessions (Thompson, 1973). Briefly, 18 unique four-response sequences were arranged in a specific order and presented to each subject one after another until the list was completed. Once completed, the same list of sequences was presented again. In this list, each of the 18 sequences occurred three to five times and adhered to the other restrictions on their ordering (i.e., adjacent positions within a sequence were different from day to day and no position within a sequence was duplicated across days). This eliminated sequence presentations such RRCL, and sequence ordering such as RLCR followed by CLRC, which would repeat the left key as the correct choice for the second position across days. Thus, each subject was exposed to a given sequence five to seven times every 108 sessions.
Delay and Delayed-Performance Training
Acquisition behavior was considered stable when response rates and the percentage of errors did not vary by more than ±20% or ±10% of the mean, respectively, for 10 consecutive sessions. This level of stability also allowed us to introduce the delay and delayed-performance phase, which was done by terminating the acquisition phase after an acquisition criterion was met. Initially, the acquisition criterion was low (e.g., one errorless sequence) and the delay was quite brief (one minute) in order to expose the subjects to these phases; however, both the acquisition criterion and the delay were incrementally increased to the terminal or baseline parameters of five errorless sequences and one hour, respectively. The one-hour delay was selected for the baseline because it allowed some time for consolidation and easily accommodated the needed pretreatment time of 30 minutes for THC injections (see below). After response rate, accuracy, and percent savings stabilized under the terminal baseline conditions for the procedure, experimental manipulations began. These manipulations included testing delays of 1 minute, 15 minutes, 1 hour, 3 hours, and 24 hours to establish a retention curve, and examining the effects of Δ9-THC at multiple delays. If the subject did not meet the criterion after 45 minutes in the acquisition phase, the session ended without progressing to the delay phase. For delays over 15 minutes in duration, subjects were returned to their home cage until just prior to the end of the delay when they were placed back in the experimental chamber. Completion of the delay was followed by the onset of the 45-minute delayed-performance phase. This phase was initiated with the illumination of the response keys and the house light, which served as a discriminative stimulus for this phase of the procedure. Throughout testing, sessions were generally conducted 5–6 days per week; Monday through Friday or Saturday.
Repeated Acquisition and Delayed-Performance Procedure
Retention and recall of the response sequences under the terminal baseline of repeated acquisition and delayed-performance was quantified using percent savings as described by Ebbinghaus (1964). This was calculated by subtracting the errors-to-criterion (ETC) in the performance phase from ETC in the acquisition phase and expressing the difference as a percentage of ETC in the acquisition phase. For example, if a rat made 100 errors in the acquisition phase before meeting the acquisition criterion, but only made 50 errors in the delayed-performance phase before meeting the same criterion, this subject would have a percent savings of 50% [(100–50/100) × 100)]. Therefore, this would indicate 50% retention, while a percent savings of zero or less would indicate no retention. No retention occurred when the ETC in the delayed-performance phase were greater than the ETC in the acquisition phase. The fact that the delayed performance phase was always 45 minutes in duration also meant that responding could extend beyond the point at which subjects met the criterion in this phase; therefore, both the percentage of errors and response rate to criterion, as well as the overall percentage of errors and response rate, were calculated for the delayed-performance phase (e.g., see the cumulative record of responding shown in Figure 7).
Ovariectomy and sham surgery:
After being trained on the previously described procedure for approximately 80 days (post-natal day 140), rats were assigned to either the ovariectomized (OVX) or intact group and underwent an ovariectomy or sham surgery, respectively. Subjects were placed under general anesthesia using i.p. injections of ketamine (50 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). Ovaries were removed using bilateral flank incisions, and the blood supply to the ovaries was tied off. Muscle walls were closed with absorbable sutures, and skin incisions closed with wound clips. Rats assigned to the intact group, underwent the same procedure excluding removal of the ovaries. Rats recovered fully within two days post-surgery. Vaginal swabs were taken to ensure OVX rats were no longer cycling. Rats that failed to show stable behavior on the procedure over the 80-day training period did not undergo surgery and were excluded from the study (n=6). In addition, OVX rats that were found to be cycling post-surgery were excluded from the study (n=3).
Drug and hormone administration:
(−)-trans-delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ9–THC) was obtained from the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) and arrived in a 5-ml ampule at a 200 mg/ml concentration dissolved in 100% ethanol. It was then divided into 20 aliquots containing 50 mg of Δ9–THC and the ethanol was evaporated by high-speed vacuum. Aliquots were stored at −20°C and were typically reconstituted to a 10 mg/ml concentration using 5 ml of a vehicle composed of 5% ethanol, 5% emulphor, and 90% saline. This concentration was also used as a stock solution for smaller concentrations as needed. Δ9–THC or Δ9–THC vehicle administration began approximately three weeks after surgery, with injections administered in a mixed order every 3 or 4 days. Both Δ9–THC vehicle and Δ9–THC were always administered acutely by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection 30 minutes prior to the start of the delayed-performance phase, regardless of the delay duration. Specific doses of Δ9–THC (0.032–3.2 mg/kg) were selected based upon previous research (e.g., Weed et al., 2016) and they were intended to comprise dose-effect curves that ranged from ineffective to maximally effective.
After a retention curve was established for each subject and dose-effect curves were determined for Δ9–THC, chronic 17β-estradiol or tamoxifen administration began. 17β-estradiol (1,3 5(10)-estrien-3,17 β-diol) was purchased from Steraloids Inc. (Newport, RI) and tamoxifen citrate was purchased from Cayman Chemical Co. (Ann Arbor, MI). Capsules containing a 25% estradiol to 75% cholesterol ratio were constructed and then implanted subcutaneously as described by Daniel et al. (2002). Similarly constructed capsules have been shown to produce estradiol levels (20–25 pg/ml) typical of the diestrus stage of the estrous cycle (Luine, 1997; Singh et al., 1994). Tamoxifen has relatively little affinity for estrogen receptors; however, it is a prodrug for receptor ligands such as afimoxifene (4-hydroxytamoxifen; 4-OHT) and endoxifene (N-desmethyl-4-hydroxytamoxifen). These metabolites have approximately 30 to 100 times greater affinity for estrogen receptors than tamoxifen itself (Ahmad et al., 2010). In order to stay consistent and to avoid the administration of a new, untested vehicle, tamoxifen was also dissolved in THC vehicle. Intact rats were subcutaneously injected with 1 mg/kg of tamoxifen at 8:00 a.m. each day of chronic administration (Kuiper et al., 1997; Velazquez-Zamora et al., 2012). The behavioral sessions for the intact females occurred 1 and 3 hours after the chronic injection. Redetermination of Δ9–THC dose-effect curves began no earlier than 10 days after capsule implantation or the start of tamoxifen administration. The injection volume for Δ9–THC and tamoxifen was 0.1 m/100 g.
Immunohistochemistry:
Brains were flash frozen immediately after removal from the skull using a commercially available coolant (Cytocool II, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Kalamazoo, MI) and stored at −80°C before being sectioned coronally on a cryostat (CM3050 S, Leica Biosystems, Buffalo Grove, IL, −18°C) and mounted onto slides. Several areas were captured in the slices for analysis, including the prefrontal cortex, nucleus accumbens, striatum, globus pallidus, hippocampus, and substantia nigra (see Winsauer et al., 2011). After sectioning, slides were stored at −80°C in a microscope slide box, which was placed in a plastic Ziploc bag until immunohistochemistry was performed. Slides were first allowed to come to room temperature before being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 45 minutes. Slides were then washed in 1x phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and blocked for 1 hour in 10% normal goat serum and 0.3% Triton-X diluted in PBS. Slides were incubated overnight at 4°C in an estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) primary antibody (rabbit: Cell Signaling (8644), Danvers, MA, 1:200)/CB1 (mouse: Synaptic Systems (258011), Germany, 1:200) solution containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA), 0.3% Triton-X and 1x PBS. Slides were washed in 1x PBS and incubated in an Alexa Flour 488 (anti-rabbit: Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, 1:200)/Alexa Flour 532 (anti-mouse: Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, 1:200) secondary antibody solution, in the dark, at room temperature for 2 hours. Slides were then washed in 1x PBS, and incubated in a DRAQ5 (Cell Signaling (4084), Danvers, MA, 1:1000) nuclei stain for 5 minutes and washed again in 1x PBS. All of the slides were cover slipped using Prolong Glass Antifade (Invitrogen, Carlsbad,CA) and allowed to cure overnight in the dark. Slides were imaged under a confocal laser microscope (Leica TCS SP8, DMi8, Leica Microsystems Inc., Buffalo Grove, IL).
Estradiol and uterine weights:
Estradiol levels were assessed using truncal blood obtained after sacrifice by decapitation. Uteruses were also dissected and weighed following sacrifice. Blood was allowed to coagulate on ice before being spun for 10 minutes at 12,000 rpm in a microcentrifuge at room temperature. After centrifugation, serum was separated into microcentrifuge tubes and stored at −80°C. Estradiol sensitive ELISA kits (DRG International, Springfield, NJ) were used for analyzing serum and the assay was performed as stated in the instructions provided (EIA-4399).
Data Analysis:
In addition to percent savings (described above), responding in the acquisition and delayed-performance phases was also characterized by overall response rate (responses/min) and the percentage of errors [(incorrect responses/total responses) × 100]. These values were determined once for the acquisition phase because this phase ended when the subject met the acquisition criterion, but they were typically determined twice during the 45-min delayed-performance phase if the subject met the criterion (i.e., before and after the criterion was met). The percentage of errors was excluded from the data analyses if overall response rate was less than 5 responses per minute due to the small number of responses emitted. Mean data for each dependent measure were analyzed using a general linear mixed-model ANOVA (IBM SPSS software, Armonk, NY) to determine the between-group (e.g., hormone) and within-group effects (Δ9–THC dose). As opposed to a repeated measures ANOVA, the mixed model method is adept at handling missing data from individual subjects (Krueger and Tian, 2004; Wainwright et al., 2007). For significant interactions or main effects, one–way ANOVA or Holm-Sidak post hoc tests were used to determine between-group and within-group differences. If a significant interaction was found, main effects were not denoted in the figure. Estradiol levels and uterine weight data were analyzed using a one-way ANOVA.
Results
Figure 3 shows the decreased retention and recall of the response sequences acquired each session as the delay duration was increased in intact and OVX female rats. These delay-dependent decreases to percent savings in both groups were also accompanied by small increases in percent errors and modest increases in the overall response rates compared to those obtained during the one-minute delay. Percent savings for the one-minute delay typically exceeded 90%, while producing mean percent errors below 20% and mean responses per minute between 30 and 40 for both to-criteria and overall responding. For percent savings, a mixed-model analysis determined there was a main effect of delay [F(4,28)=68.97, p<0.001, η2 =0.74] and a OVX x delay interaction [F(4,28)=3.26, p<0.05, η2 =0.27 ]. Post hoc comparisons indicated that percent savings at all delay durations were significantly different from the one-minute delay in both intact and OVX rats (Fig. 3, panel A). Although there was no main effect of OVX, the difference between OVX and intact rats approached significance at the 24-hour delay [F(1,18)=4.04, p=0.059, d=0.99].
With respect to the increases in percent errors, there was a main effect of OVX [F(1,15)=4.88, p<0.05, η2 =0.28] and delay [F(4,27)=17.99, p<0.001, η2 =0.37], and a OVX x delay interaction [F(4,27)=2.94, p<0.05, η2 =0.26] for percent errors to criteria (Fig. 3, panel B). A main effect of delay [F(4,41)=17.04, p<0.001 η2 =0.36, ] and a OVX x delay interaction [F(4,41)=4.07, p<0.01, η2 =0.27] was also seen in overall percent errors (Fig. 3, panel C). For intact rats, post-hoc comparisons found a significant difference from the one-minute delay at the 24-hour delay duration for percent errors to criteria (Fig. 3, panel B), and at the 15-minute, 1-hour and 24-hour delay duration for overall percent errors (Fig. 3, panel C). For OVX rats, all delay durations were found to be significantly different from the one-minute delay for percent errors to criteria and overall percent errors (Fig. 3, panels B and C).
There was only a main effect of delay for response rate to criteria [F(4,38)=12.89, p<0.001, η2 =0.34] and overall response rate [F(4,61)=12.81, p<0.001, η2 =0.33]. Therefore, the higher overall rates obtained for the intact group were not significantly different from those for the OVX group, and the significant increases that occurred after 15 min, 1 hour, and 3 hours in comparison to the one-minute delay applied to both groups (Fig. 3, panel E).
Figure 4 shows the disruptive effects of acute doses of Δ9–THC on retention and recall in intact and OVX female rats during a 1-hour delay. Moreover, when compared to Δ9–THC vehicle administration, these dose-dependent effects were comparable in both intact and OVX females at all of the doses except the 0.56 mg/kg dose where the decrease in percent savings was greater for intact group than the OVX group [F(1,39)=5.49, p<0.05, d =1.15] (Fig. 4, panel A). These decreases in percent savings were also accompanied by increases in percent errors and decreases in response rates. This was verified for percent savings using a mixed-model analysis, which indicated there was a main effect of Δ9–THC dose [F(5,18)=71.39, p<0.001, η2 =0.75] and an OVX x dose interaction [F(5,18)=2.91, p<0.05, η2 =0.26]. For both intact and OVX rats, post-hoc comparisons found significant decreases from control (Δ9–THC vehicle) in percent savings at doses from 0.56 to 3.2 mg/kg Δ9–THC. These same doses also produced a main effect of dose for percent errors [F(5,17)=38.42, p<0.001, η2 =0.52] and response rate to criteria [F(5,44)=77.46, p<0.001, η2 =0.80] and overall percent errors [F(5,14)=29.66, p<0.001, η2 =0.45-](Fig 4, panels B-D). For overall response rate, a main effect of dose [F(5,45)=28.28, p<0.001, η2 =0.44] was only produced by doses of 1–3.2 mg/kg Δ9–THC (Fig. 4, panel E).
The results depicted in Figures 5 and 6 show the extent to which Δ9–THC in both intact and OVX female rats, respectively, produced dose- and delay-dependent effects after delays of one and three hours. To make this comparison, the data collected with vehicle and two doses of Δ9–THC after a one-hour delay were compared with data for vehicle and the same doses after a three-hour delay. In intact females, the analysis indicated there were both dose- and delay-dependent effects, as there were larger effects on retention for both doses of Δ9–THC after the 3-hour delay [F(1,30)=11.14, p<0.01, d =1.93] [F(1,9)=6.64, p<0.05, d =6.637] and 0.56 mg/kg significantly decreased percent savings from control after both delays (Fig. 5, panel A). This was supported statistically by a main effect of delay [F(1,29)=9.07, p<0.01, η2 =0.46], dose [F(2,5)=16.89, p<0.01, η2 =0.57] and a delay x dose interaction [F(2,10)=5.36, p<0.05, η2 =0.41] for percent savings.
For percent errors to criteria (Fig. 5, panel B), there was a main effect of delay [F(1,5)=15.98, p<0.05, η2 =0.56] and Δ9–THC dose [F(2,5)=11.16, p<0.05, η2 =0.49], but there were no significant main effects nor an interaction for overall percent errors (Fig. 5, panel C). For response rate to criteria in the intact group (Fig. 5, panel D), the analyses identified a main effect of delay [F(1,13)=14.07, p<0.01, η2 =0.53] and dose [F(2,6)=7.50, p<0.05, η2 =0.44]. Lastly, for overall response rate, there was a main effect of delay [F(1,5)=15.35, p<0.05, η2 =0.55] and a delay x dose interaction [F(2,4)=13.10, p<0.05, η2 =0.52], which resulted from a significant increase in response rate compared to the vehicle control and a significant difference between delays at a dose of 0.32 mg/kg [F(1,3)=24.79, p<0.05, d =2.87] (Fig. 5, panel E).
As shown in Figure 6, Δ9–THC produced a dose-dependent reduction in retention and recall in OVX rats that was similar to that obtained in intact rats. However, unlike the effects in intact females, the effects of Δ9–THC were not delay-dependent in OVX rats. This was evident from the statistical analyses, which indicated there were only main effects of delay [F(1,26)=5.75, p<0.05, η2 =0.34] and dose [F(2,7)=10.20, p<0.01], but no delay x dose interaction for percent savings (Fig. 6, panel A). The diminished role of delay in the effects of Δ9–THC in OVX rats was also evident from the analyses of percent errors to criteria (Fig. 6, panel B), where there was only a significant main effect of dose [F(2,3)=9.36, p<0.05, η2 =0.38]; the main effect of delay and the interaction were not significant. For overall percent errors, there were no significant main effects or interaction (Fig. 6, panel C).
In contrast to percent errors, there were effects of delay [F(1,28)=20.25, p<0.001, η2 =0.49] and dose [F(2,9)=29.75, p<0.001, η2 =0.59] in the response rate to criteria, as well as a significant delay x dose interaction [F(2,7)=7.32, p<0.05, η2 =0.36]. Post-hoc analysis of these data found a significant decrease from control at both doses of Δ9–THC and a significant difference between the 1- and 3-hour delay data after both doses [F(1,5)=8.32, p<0.05, d =1.54] [F(1,30)=10.0, p<0.01, d =1.69] (Fig. 6, panel D). However, there were no significant main effects nor an interaction for overall response rate in OVX rats (Fig. 6, panel E).
Figure 7 shows the increased disruption of within-session responding produced by 0.56 mg/kg of Δ9– THC when the delay between acquisition and delayed performance was increased from one to three hours in an intact female rat. The records in the top row depict sequence retention after one hour. On this day, the subject made 60 errors before meeting the acquisition criterion of 5 errorless sequences, and only 34 errors before meeting the same criterion after administration of a 0.56-mg/kg dose of Δ9–THC, 30 minutes before the delayed-performance phase began. Figure 8 shows the effects of Δ9–THC on one-hour retention and recall in OVX rats before and after chronic 17β-estradiol implantation. When compared with vehicle administration, which produced similar percent savings under both conditions (54.2 and 55.2%), chronic 17β-estradiol reduced the disruptive effects of Δ9–THC on retention and recall. This was evident from the upward shift in the Δ9–THC dose-effect curve for percent saving at the higher doses after implantation, as well as the significant 17β-estradiol x Δ9–THC dose interaction [F(5,17)=7.03, p<0.01, η2 =0.36]. Moreover, post hoc analyses indicated that doses of 0.56–3.2 mg/kg produced significant decreases in percent savings compared to vehicle prior to estradiol implantation, but no dose of Δ9–THC significantly decreased percent savings compared to vehicle after implantation. In addition, percent savings after administration of 1 and 1.8 mg/kg of Δ9–THC was significantly higher after estradiol implantation than before implantation [F(1,8)=15.44, p<0.01, d =2.10] [F(1,16)=6.36, p<0.05, d =1.35] (Fig. 8, panel A). In terms of main effects, Δ9–THC dose was significant [F(5,13)=216.69, p<0.001, η2 =2.50], but 17β-estradiol was not [F(1,6)=4.06, p=0.09, η2 =0.33].
As shown in the lower panels of Figure 8, mean percent errors to criterion were similar for vehicle injections during both time periods (23 versus 20.3%), and all of the doses of Δ9–THC significantly increased percent errors to criterion under both 17β-estradiol conditions (Fig. 8, panel B). Unlike the statistical analyses for percent savings, however, 17β-estradiol produced a significant main effect [F(1,37)=20.25, p<.0.001, η2 =0.49] and Δ9-THC dose [F(5,12)=76.70, p<.0.001, η2 =1.07], but the 17β-estradiol x Δ9–THC dose interaction was not significant [F(5,10)=2.74, p=0.084, η2 =0.31]. When overall percent errors were analyzed, there was a 17β-estradiol x Δ9–THC dose interaction [F(5,5)=58.80, p<0.001, η2 =0.89], in addition to a main effect of Δ9–THC dose [F(5,9)=23.70, p<0.001, η2 =0.53], but no main effect of 17β-estradiol [F(1,12)=2.651, p=0.130, η2 =0.312]. Overall percent errors prior to estradiol implantation were significantly greater than vehicle at doses of 1–3.2 mg/kg Δ9–THC, whereas after implantation none of the doses were significantly decreased compared to vehicle (Fig. 8, panel C). Although quite small, pairwise comparisons also indicated there was a significant difference between the two 17β-estradiol conditions after 0.32 mg/kg dose of Δ9–THC [F(1,60)=7.71, p<0.01, d =1.48] (Fig. 8, panel C). The statistical differences obtained for percent errors to criterion and overall percent errors, particularly at the lower doses of Δ9–THC, was another indication of how responding after the criterion was met in the delayed-performance phase could affect these two measures of accuracy.
With respect to response rate (Fig. 8, panels D and E), chronic 17β-estradiol implantation (1) decreased baseline response rates as indicated by the significant differences obtained after vehicle administration during the two conditions, and (2) decreased the potency of Δ9–THC’s rate-decreasing effects. These effects were evident statistically from the significant main effects of Δ9–THC for the to-criterion [F(5,13)=50.52, p=0.001, η2 =0.80] and overall [F(5,12)=124.14, p<0.001, η2 =1.55] response rate data, as well as the significant 17β-estradiol x Δ9–THC dose interactions for both the response rate to criterion [F(5,11)=333.87, p<0.001, η2 =3.69] and the overall response rate [F(5,14)=30.20, p<0.001, η2 =0.59]. Likely due to these interactions there were no main effects of 17β-estradiol on the response rate to criterion [F(1,14)=0.78, p=0.39, η2 =0.29] or the overall response rates [F(1,6)=4.52, p=0.08, η2 =0.33]. Nevertheless, pairwise comparisons confirmed 17β-estradiol-induced decreases in the to-criterion and overall response rates after vehicle administration [F(1,6)=17.06, p<0.01, d =2.21] and in overall response rates after the administration of 0.32–0.56 mg/kg of Δ9–THC [F(1,11)=7.03, p<0.05, d =1.42] [F(1,7)=6.40, p<0.05, d =1.35]. Pairwise comparisons also confirmed the reduction in potency in that 0.56 mg/kg of Δ9–THC did not significantly decrease response rate to criterion (Fig. 8, panel D) during chronic 17β-estradiol and 1–1.8 mg/kg did not significantly decrease overall response rates (Fig. 8, panel E).

Figure 10 compares the initial, pre-chronic effects of Δ9–THC on retention and recall after a delay of one hour with the effects of Δ9–THC after the same delay during daily administration of 1 mg/kg of tamoxifen in the intact group. During both time periods, vehicle injections served as a control for Δ9–THC injections, and as a control for any changes in the baseline behavior. As shown, chronic tamoxifen did not significantly alter mean percent savings (56.5 versus 61%) or mean percent errors (to criteria: 24.9 versus 21.7%; overall: 16 versus 14.1%), but it did significantly decrease mean response rate (to criteria: 43.5 versus 31 responses/minute; overall: 53.9 versus 36.5 responses/minute).
Given that the disruptive effects of Δ9–THC on percent saving (Fig. 10, panel A) were similar, the only effect identified by the mixed-model analysis was the main effect of Δ9–THC dose [F(5,17)=67.0, p<0.001, η2 =0.54]. For percent errors to criteria (Fig. 10, panel B), there was a main effect of Δ9–THC dose [F(5,11)=105.53, p<0.001, η2 =0.73] and a tamoxifen x Δ9–THC dose interaction [F(5,19)=4.69, p<0.01, η2 =0.22]. Analysis of data for overall percent errors (Fig. 10, panel C) revealed only a main effect of Δ9–THC dose [F(5,19)=69.15, p<0.001, η2 =0.55]. For response rate to criteria (Fig. 10, panel D), there was a main effect of chronic tamoxifen [F(1,9)=10.1, p<0.05, η2 =0.25], Δ9–THC dose [F(5,18)=39.57, p<0.001, η2 =0.4] and a tamoxifen x Δ9–THC dose interaction [F(5,28)=4.20, p<0.01, η2 =0.22]. As shown, response rate to criteria during chronic tamoxifen was lower than pre-chronic rates after vehicle [F(1,25)=22.72, p<0.001, d =2.13] and the two lowest doses of Δ9–THC (i.e., 0.32 and 0.56 mg/kg) [F(1,31)=11.11, p<0.01, d =1.49] [F(1,10)=6.18, p<0.05, d =1.11], and was less potently decreased compared to control (i.e., only 1.8 and 3.2 mg/kg of Δ9–THC were significantly different from control). For overall response rate, there was a main effect of chronic tamoxifen [F(1,14)=20.77, p<0.001, η2 =0.30] and Δ9–THC dose [F(5,14)=25.27, p<0.001, η2 =0.33], but no tamoxifen x Δ9–THC dose interaction (Fig. 10, panel E).

Discussion
The results from these studies demonstrated that increasing delays from 1 minute to 24 hours decreased retention and recall comparably in OVX and intact adult female rats. Moreover, with the exception of one dose (0.56 mg/kg), Δ9-THC similarly disrupted retention and recall in both OVX and intact females when the delay after acquisition (learning) was one hour. The disruptive effects of Δ9-THC became more hormone-dependent, however, when the delay duration was increased from one to three hours, as increasing the delay increased the potency of Δ9-THC’s disruptive effects more in the intact than OVX females. The present results also demonstrated that implanting OVX females with 17β-estradiol reduced the disruptive effects of Δ9-THC when the delay was one hour, and that these effects in the OVX+E group were accompanied by increased estradiol levels and uterine weights compared with OVX females. In addition, a qualitative assessment of hippocampal slices taken from a single subject in each of the 3 hormone groups (intact, OVX, OVX+E) showed an increase in the levels of ERα receptors in the OVX and OVX+E females, as compared to the levels of CB1 receptors. Finally, the capacity of chronic 17β-estradiol to alter the disruptive effects of Δ9-THC on retention and recall were in contrast to those for chronic tamoxifen administration in intact females, which did not alter Δ9-THC’s effects on memory even though it produced a decrease in response rate.
From a broad perspective, the present results with intact and OVX rats are consistent with previous preclinical research (Amato et al., 2011; Bolhuis et al., 1986; Hackett et al., 2015; Niyuhire et al., 2007) showing that retention of task-based information decays with time after the information has been acquired (see Figure 3). Moreover, these results were very similar to those of Amato et al. (2011) using the same procedure with male rats, as they demonstrated significant reductions in retention and recall 30 minutes after acquisition when compared to a 5-min delay. Where our results differed from some of the existing literature is that we did not find a significant difference on retention between OVX and intact females. In studies examining the effects of hormone status alone on memory, intact female rats have been shown to perform better than OVX females on memory-associated tasks such as the Morris water maze, object recognition, object placement, and trial-unique nonmatching-to-location (Sbisa et al, 2017; Wallace et al, 2006; Sarkaki et al, 2008; Su et al, 2012). In our study, there was only a non-significant trend for intact females to do better at longer delays than OVX females. The fact that intact and OVX females did not significantly differ for responding under the repeated acquisition and delayed-performance procedure raises several questions, including the lack of effect of the estrous cycle.
The 4–5 day rat estrous cycle is comprised of 4 phases: diestrus I, diestrus II, proestrus and estrus. The most wide-ranging hormonal changes take place during the proestrus phase, when estradiol and progesterone levels peak, and are followed by a substantial decrease in the estrus phase (Becker et al., 2005). Naturally-cycling female rats have been shown to use a hippocampally-dependent place strategy during proestrus, and a striatally-dependent response strategy during estrus in order to find food in a T-maze task (Korol et al., 2004). Also, female rats trained on a place version of the Morris water maze performed better during proestrus than the estrus phase (Warren and Juraska, 1997). Estrogen is believed to be responsible for these changes, as estradiol-treated OVX rats performed better on a hippocampally-dependent place task and worse on a striatally-dependent response task than untreated OVX rats (Davis et al., 2005). That said, a complex task requiring subjects to use both place (key position) and response strategies (sequence) such as ours (cf. Winsauer et al., 2011) might obscure the influence of cyclic hormonal changes compared to tasks only requiring one type of response strategy.
Acute administration of Δ9–THC after sequence acquisition and during an hour delay produced comparable dose-dependent increases to percent errors and decreases to percent savings and response rate in both intact and OVX rats (see Figure 4). These results are similar to other studies in which Δ9–THC administration after task acquisition impaired recall of the target zone location in mice trained on the Morris water-maze procedure and decreased step-through latency in rats trained on a passive avoidance procedure (Mishima et al., 2001; Niyuhire et al., 2007). Interestingly, these preclinical findings, which indicate that Δ9–THC can impair the retention of information learned under a non-drug state, conflict with several of the early memory studies involving humans. For example, in the 1970s, a series of experiments examining the effects of marijuana on memory reported that marijuana disrupted retrieval when administered prior to learning, but not after (Abel, 1971a; b; Darley et al., 1973; Miller et al., 1977). More recently, Ranganathan et al. (2017) obtained similar results and concluded that intravenous Δ9–THC administration impaired encoding, but not the retrieval of verbal information learned in a non-drug state. These studies, however, do not necessarily contradict what preclinical data have shown; namely, that information under weak stimulus control (i.e., not yet learned) is easier to disrupt than information that has come under strong stimulus control (i.e., been rehearsed) (Thompson and Moerschbaecher, 1979). Thus, a dose of drug administered before learning will be more disruptive to the recall of task information than that same dose administered after the learning and encoding phases, and single dose drug studies (clinical or preclinical) could inadvertently misinterpret these potency differences as differences in effect. One also has to consider the possibility that drug administration prior to the learning phase could be more disruptive simply because it has the capacity to disrupt multiple memory processes (e.g., acquisition, encoding, and retrieval depending on the delay). This latter possibility is also one of the reasons the present studies began by examining the retrieval and recall of information acquired in the non-drug state. We also did not want THC-induced sedation to affect sequence acquisition. Although comparing decreases in locomotor activity with decreases in food-motivated operant responding is difficult, Tseng and Craft (2001) have shown that higher doses of Δ9–THC (3–10 mg/kg) can decrease locomotor activity and produce cataplexy in female rats.
Although the effects of Δ9–THC on retention and recall were largely similar in intact and OVX females when the delay was one hour, there were clear instances when it was significantly more potent at disrupting retention and recall in intact females than OVX females. The first instance was at the 0.56-mg/kg dose during the one hour delay (Figure 4) and the second instance was at the 0.32-mg/kg dose when the delay was increased to three hours (Figure 5). These effects systematically replicated previous findings from our lab showing that intact female rats can be more sensitive than OVX female rats to the disruptive effects of Δ9–THC (Winsauer et al., 2011; Winsauer and Sutton, 2014). Paradoxically then, hormonally-intact females seem to have an advantage over OVX females in memory tasks unless they have been administered Δ9–THC. The sensitivity of hormonally-intact females to the disruptive effects of Δ9–THC also does not support our data in Daniel et al. (2002) and the present study showing that 17β-estradiol implantation in OVX females can antagonize these effects. As shown in Figure 8, chronic 17β-estradiol administration using subcutaneous capsules attenuated the disruptive effects of Δ9–THC on sequence retention, response rate and accuracy in OVX female rats, and yielded estradiol levels that were significantly higher than those observed in intact rats. Estradiol has also been found to attenuate the disruptive effects of intrahippocampally-administered scopolamine in OVX rats trained on a delayed matching-to-position T-maze task (Gibbs, 1999). In a reinforced T-maze alteration task, estradiol administration to OVX females blocked the scopolamine-induced decreases in correct alterations (Fader et al., 1998). Together, these findings demonstrate that chronic estradiol has the capacity to attenuate both cannabinoid-induced and anticholinergic-induced disruptions of hippocampally-mediated memories.
Why this antagonism does not appear to extend, or at least partially extend, to intact females remains an important question both experimentally and clinically. Obvious factors include the duration of 17β-estradiol administration (continuous versus cyclic), the role of other sex hormones such as progesterone, and the age at which 17β-estradiol is administered. Consider the interaction between estrogen and progesterone, for example. Whereas estrogen has been shown to enhance memory, the addition of progesterone has been found to oppose or reverse those enhancements. When administered in combination with estradiol, progesterone impaired spatial memory and reversed the positive effects of estrogen on Morris water maze performance in OVX female rats (Bimonte-Nelson et al., 2006; Chesler and Juraska, 2000). As opposed to estrogen, progesterone has also been found to decrease dendritic spine density in the hippocampus (Woolley and McEwen, 1993). While the interactive effects of hormone status and Δ9–THC on memory in female rats appear to be largely mediated by estrogen, progesterone likely plays some role in mediating estrogen’s effects.
The role of other hormones and their respective signaling mechanisms may also explain why the administration of tamoxifen did not alter the effects of Δ9–THC. As shown in Figure 10, the Δ9–THC-induced disruptions to sequence retention (i.e., percent savings) prior to tamoxifen were essentially the same as those obtained during chronic tamoxifen. Similar to our results, chronic tamoxifen administration did not significantly affect performance of intact rats trained on a Morris water maze and a passive avoidance task (Zabihi et al., 2014). While the lack of significant effects on retention could be related to the choice of dosage or the specificity of this dosage, tamoxifen did produce significant rate-decreasing effects, which indicates it was behaviorally effective. Initially, Δ9–THC was thought to have estrogenic properties, but early investigations found that Δ9–THC did not interact with the estrogen receptor or compete with estradiol binding to the receptor (Okey and Bondy, 1977; Sauer et al., 1983). However, a more recent study by Takeda et al. (2013) found Δ9–THC may be anti-estrogenic through an upregulation of ERβ, which leads to a downregulation of ERα and consequent disruption of 17β-estradiol/ERα signaling. These results suggest a less direct interaction with estrogen receptors than was previously indicated. In contrast to Δ9–THC, administration of estradiol has been found to significantly increase ERα, but not affect ERβ in the hippocampus of OVX female rats (Foster, 2012; Rodgers et al., 2010). This estradiol-induced increase in ERα directly supports qualitative observations from our immunohistochemcial slides (Figure 11), which show a higher density of ERα in an estradiol-implanted OVX rat. Thus, ERα may play a critical role in mediating the effects of Δ9–THC on memory in female rats, and future studies will need to more carefully target their potential role in the observed antagonism between Δ9–THC and 17β-estradiol.
In conclusion, this study extends previous preclinical findings indicating that Δ9–THC has the capacity to disrupt the retention of sequential information/responding acquired in a non-drug state in both intact and OVX female rats. Further, the present findings show that the disruptive effects of Δ9–THC on retention in OVX rats were attenuated by estradiol implantation and suggest a possible role for ERα receptors. The effects of Δ9–THC in intact female rats, on the other hand, were insensitive to chronic administration of the non-specific estrogen receptor modifier tamoxifen, suggesting a more complex interaction between Δ9–THC and the estrous cycle that may be due to the presence of progesterone. These findings add to the understanding of Δ9–THC’s sex-dependent effects on learning and memory, and highlight the need for more investigation into the underlying mechanisms that produce these effects as use of the cannabinoids expands exponentially.
