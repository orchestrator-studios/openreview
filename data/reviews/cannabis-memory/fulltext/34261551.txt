Introduction
Addiction is a multifaceted disorder involving complex interactions between neurobiological, psychological, and behavioural components.1 Hazardous drinking describes alcohol consumption patterns that raise the probability of experiencing adverse consequences.2 Despite significant advances in understanding addiction, its heterogeneous nature remains a critical challenge for both diagnosis and treatment.3 Traditional diagnostic frameworks, such as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), focus primarily on symptomatic presentations while often overlooking the underlying psychological and neurobiological mechanisms driving addictive behaviours.4,5 Although this symptom-based approach is useful in clinical practice, it is atheoretical, does not address etiological determinants, and, as a result, does not directly inform intervention approaches.
The Addictions Neuroclinical Assessment (ANA) framework offers a promising alternative by categorizing addictions into three core domains: executive function, negative emotionality, and incentive salience.6 These domains provide a more comprehensive perspective on the neurobiological and psychological underpinnings of addiction, enabling precise assessments and personalized treatment approaches. Unlike traditional frameworks, the ANA adopts a dimensional and mechanistic approach, offering the potential to deepen understanding of addiction and its diverse manifestations.7
Despite its theoretical promise, empirical research on the ANA framework is relatively nascent and there are notable gaps. Early validation studies have primarily focused on treatment-seeking individuals with alcohol use disorder,8–11 which has advanced the measurement of ANA domains but also restricts generalizability to clinical populations. More recently, however, there has been a growing body of work applying the ANA framework in non-treatment-seeking and community-based samples, including problem drinkers and heavy drinkers.12–17 These studies highlight the feasibility of extending the ANA beyond clinical treatment contexts, offering critical insights into early-stage and heterogeneous patterns of alcohol involvement. Nevertheless, the overall empirical landscape remains limited, underscoring the need for additional multimodal validation in diverse populations to enhance the framework's robustness and applicability. The present study builds on this emerging line of work by leveraging two independent non-treatment-seeking samples to test the dimensional validity of the ANA domains across the spectrum of alcohol use.17 In addition, validating ANA in young adults would be particularly beneficial, as this age group is marked by increased engagement in risky drinking behaviours and the highest population prevalence of hazardous drinking, and substantive vulnerability to developing persistent alcohol problems across the lifespan.18 Finally, novel contemporary computational approaches, such as machine learning, analyse multidimensional data to uncover patterns and relationships that traditional methods may miss and have not been applied to the ANA framework.19 These techniques highlight the unique contributions of ANA domains, and by handling high-dimensional, intercorrelated data, computational approaches can reveal complex multivariate patterns and enhance predictive accuracy beyond conventional regression models. This may improve screening precision, refine dimensional assessment, and ultimately support more targeted interventions, making computational validation a critical step in advancing the framework's research and clinical applications.20
This study aims to computationally validate the ANA framework in relation to hazardous drinking by leveraging machine learning models to identify key features within the domains of executive function, negative emotionality, and incentive salience. This approach attempts to not only facilitates a deeper understanding of addiction's underlying mechanisms but also support more precise identification of risk factors and potential treatment targets. The findings from this validation study may serve as a foundation for refining addictions assessments, tailoring interventions, and capturing the heterogeneity inherent in the alcohol drinking behaviour.
Methods
Participants
This was a retrospective analysis of two separately collected datasets. The first dataset consisted of a cohort of 1435 non-clinical adults from the general community (Mean ± SD age = 39.81 ± 13.68), who participated in a single in-person assessment as part of a research registry at St. Joseph's Healthcare in Hamilton, Ontario, with broad eligibility criteria to ensure inclusivity (i.e., age 18–65, willingness to be contacted for future studies, and using a computer (including smartphones) at least weekly to ensure competence with electronic assessments). the behavioural economic predictors of changes in risky alcohol consumption.
The second dataset used in this study comes from a longitudinal observational cohort of 721 young adult binge drinkers (mean age = 21.43, SD = 1.18). Participants were recruited from the community in Hamilton, Ontario, through print and online advertisements. The cohort was originally designed to examine behavioural economic predictors of changes in risky alcohol consumption.21 Eligibility criteria required participants to be between 19.5 and 23 years old and to report binge drinking (≥3 drinks per episode for females or ≥4 for males) at least twice monthly, or at least once monthly when accompanied by weekly or more frequent cannabis use, given the elevated alcohol-related harms associated with concurrent use.22
Ethical approvals for both studies were granted by the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (HiREB #4699 and #2193, respectively). See Table 1 for complete demographic details after implementing the quality control criteria described in the quality control section.

Measures
The principal dependent variable was the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT), which was developed by the Word Health Organization (WHO) and is widely used globally and extensively validated.23–25

To capture the executive function domain, the measures included the digit span task,26 Go/No-Go task accuracy,27 delayed reward discounting as measured by the Immediate Choice Ratio (ICR) of the extended Monetary Choice Questionnaire (MCQ),28,29 the World Health Organization Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS),30 and UPPS-P subscales for premeditation and perseverance,31 as well as all subscales from Barratt Impulsiveness Scale (BIS).32 To capture the negative emotionality domain, the measures included depressive symptoms as measured by the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9),33 post-traumatic stress symptoms as measured by (PCL-5),34 UPPS-P subscales for negative and positive urgency,31 and the Drinking Motive Questionnaire (DMQ; Cooper, 1994) coping subscale. To capture incentive salience, the measures consisted of UPPS-P sensation seeking,31 Alcohol Purchase Task (APT)35 indices including breakpoint, Omax, alpha, and intensity, along with DMQ subscales for enhancement and social motives.36 Variables were assigned to the ANA domain they most conceptually matched, which either directly mapped to a previously conducted factor analysis16 or theoretically aligned to the original framework.6 In cases of potential overlap (e.g., urgency facets relevant to both negative emotionality and executive function), variables were assigned to the domain judged most theoretically aligned to ensure independence of variance explained. Overlaps were intentionally avoided to isolate domain-specific contributions. Quality control
For the APT, quality control was ensured by removing participants who met any of the following criteria: a trend violation, indicating insufficient decrease in consumption as price increased; a global bounce violation, where consumption at any price point exceeded consumption at the initial price; a local bounce violation, where consumption at a higher price surpassed that of the preceding lower price; and a reversal violation, where consumption returned to nonzero levels after reaching zero at a higher price, reflecting unexpected reversals in purchasing behaviour.37 Generally, APT quality control criteria were applied to identify non-systematic data. Those with ΔQ values (log-unit reductions) less than 0.025 were deemed to have non-systematic data. In addition, more than 5% of consumption values that were 1.25 higher than the consumption at $0 were deemed non-systematic. Similarly, more than 10% of consumption values at price p, which reflected an increase in consumption compared to consumption at price p – 1 were deemed non-systematic. Finally, any reversals from 0 consumption were also deemed non-systematic. After quality control, the APT indices (i.e., breakpoint, alpha, intensity, and Omax) were reduced to components using oblique principal component analysis (PCA) due to their high correlation. This approach was chosen as these indices have been shown to exhibit a robust two-factor latent structure.38 For the Go/No-Go task, participants demonstrating low effort, defined as omission errors exceeding 50% or commission errors exceeding 80%, were removed.39 The MCQ delay discounting task included three trials with a control question at the end of each trial. In each of the three control questions (one for each magnitude), participants were presented with two rewards without any delay, with the expectation that they would consistently favour the higher reward. Participants who did not answer the control question correctly had their corresponding ICR value omitted, and participants who answered fewer than two out of three control questions correctly were removed. For the Digit Span Task, quality control was applied to assess effort, with participants scoring less than 2 on forward or backward spans, or fewer than 6 across both, excluded from further analysis.
In the non-clinical community adult sample, 2.75% (36 individuals) of the data were excluded due to APT violations, 7.57% (99 individuals) for low effort on the go/no-go task, 1.68% (22 individuals) for low effort on the digit span task, and 0.15% (2 individuals) for failing the control question in delay discounting, 1.2% (16 individuals) with AUDIT scores of 0 were excluded to focus analyses on the continuum of drinkers, resulting in a cumulative data loss of 10.99% and a final sample size of 1260. In the young adult clinical sample, 1.11% (8 individuals) were excluded for APT violations, 7.35% (53 individuals) for low effort on the go/no-go task, and 0.69% (5 individuals) for low effort on the digit span task, leading to a cumulative data loss of 9.15% and a final sample size of 655. A sensitivity analysis was conducted for both samples, comparing excluded and included participants in terms of demographics and the outcome variable (see Table S2).
Computational models
The categorisation of variables into the ANA domains enabled a structured approach to analysing each domain's relationship with AUDIT. For each domain in both datasets, we independently trained four machine learning models: elastic net (EN),40 random forest (RF),41 support vector machine (SVM),42 and gradient boosting machine (GB).43 The predictions from the best-performing model in each of the three domains were input into a meta-learner, an independent model that follow the type of the base models (i.e., EN, RF, GB, or SVM). This meta-learner integrated predictions across domains, producing a comprehensive model that combined information from all three ANA domains. See Fig. 1 for the methodological pipeline visualisation.
The methodological pipeline. Note: panel (a) illustrates the nested cross-validation process; panel (b) depicts the methodological pipeline in which the ANA domains (i.e., executive function, negative function, and incentive salience) are validated and combined using four different machine learning algorithms: elastic net, support vector machine, random forest, and gradient boosting machine.
Each dataset was split into 80% for training and 20% for a “holdout” testing set,44 with the train–test split stratified by age, sex, and AUDIT score to preserve distributions, and model training performed using nested five-fold cross-validation model training performed using nested five-fold cross-validation. The outer loop of the cross-validation estimated the model performance, while the inner loop estimated the best hyperparameters using a grid search process,45 with the following configurations explored for each model: For the random forest, the parameters included the number of estimators (100, 200, 300), maximum depth (None, 10, 20, 30), minimum samples split (2, 5, 10), minimum samples leaf (1, 2, 4), and whether to use bootstrapping (True, False). For gradient boosting, parameters included the number of estimators (100, 200), learning rate (0.01, 0.1, 0.2), maximum depth (3, 4, 5), and minimum samples split (2, 5, 10). The elastic net model was tuned for alpha (0.1, 1.0, 10.0), l1 ratio (0.1, 0.5, 0.9), and maximum iterations (1000, 5000). Finally, for the support vector machine, the parameters included C (0.1, 1.0, 10.0), gamma (“scale,” “auto”), and epsilon (0.1, 0.2, 0.5). This nested five-fold cross-validation process, combined with the initial 80/20 train/test split, was implemented to minimize bias, reduce the risk of overfitting, enhance generalizability, and improve the validity and reliability of the model's evaluation estimates.46 The initial training and “holdout” test sets were balanced on demographics (i.e., sex and age) and the output variable (i.e., AUDIT score) using stratified sampling.
The R2 and mean square error (MSE) on both the nested cross-validation training and validation sets (average of the nested five-cross-validation) as well as “holdout” testing set were reported and the best performing model were indicated by using the R2 value “holdout” testing set. All models were implemented in Python using Scikit-Learn.47 To further assess predictive power across domains, feature importance analysis was conducted for each domain-specific model as well as for the meta-learner using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) scores and the percentage of explained variance for each feature.48 This analysis provided insight into the contributions of different variables, enhancing our understanding of domain-specific influences within the ANA framework. Finally, to better understand the relationships between variables within each domain, zero-order correlation matrices were generated. While all computational models employed are robust to multicollinearity, the matrices were used to identify and condense variables with a Pearson's correlation coefficient of ≥0.71 (i.e., R2 ≥ 0.51, more shared than unique variance) via PCA. This minimized any residual effects of multicollinearity on the models and enhance the interpretability of feature importance and was only applied to the sub-facets of the APT, as is common.38,49–51 PCA was conducted independently within each fold during cross-validation and separately on the “holdout” test set to minimise any risk of data leakage.

Discussion
This study used machine learning to computationally examine the ANA framework in relation to hazardous drinking by utilising two datasets encompassing different age groups. Of the models examined, elastic net was identified as the most effective machine learning model for predicting AUDIT scores, with incentive salience contributing the highest proportion of explained variance in both adult and young adult groups. Negative emotionality and executive function demonstrated significant but secondary contributions, underscoring the multidimensional nature of addiction-related constructs. However, negative emotionality was significantly more implicated compared to executive function. This disparity may stem from executive function being more relevant to older or higher-severity samples, where the cumulative neurotoxic effects of alcohol on the brain become more pronounced.52,53
The dominance of incentive salience highlights its pivotal role in driving hazardous drinking, consistent with theories linking reward sensitivity to addiction. Additionally, the incentive salience measures were primarily alcohol-specific, while the other domains relied on domain-general measures, increasing its comparative predictive power. The significance of DMQ components and APT metrics underscores how motivations and reward valuation shape the severity of alcohol use and hazardous drinking.54 Differences in domain contributions suggest varying mechanisms underlying addiction across age groups and populations.55 The prominence of incentive salience across age groups in predicting AUDIT scores underscores the significant role of reward-driven behaviours in addiction. This concept aligns with the Incentive Sensitization Theory of Addiction, which posits that repeated alcohol use enhances the motivational value of alcohol-related cues, leading to compulsive seeking behaviours.56–58 Similarly, this aligns with behavioural economic models, such as reinforcer pathology and contextualized reinforcer pathology, which emphasize the heightened reinforcing value of alcohol as a central feature of hazardous drinking. These models focus less on compulsive use and instead highlight how elevated alcohol demand, particularly in certain environmental or contextual conditions, serves as a key predictor of problematic drinking behaviour.59,60 It is important to note that the dominance of incentive salience in this study is likely influenced by the relatively low-risk drinking nature of the samples, consistent with ANA theory that negative emotionality and executive function become more prominent with more severe, chronic alcohol involvement. Unlike prior studies in which depression emerged as a central feature of negative emotionality,8,16 the PHQ-9 contributed only minimally in the present analysis. This likely reflects the nature of our community-based, non-clinical samples, where depressive symptoms were generally mild, thereby limiting variance explained relative to treatment-seeking cohorts.
The subtle differences in feature importance between the two investigated samples may be attributed to developmental and contextual influences. For example, in the executive function domain, perseverance and motor component of the BIS scale emerged as leading predictors in the young adult sample, whereas these features were less critical in the adult group. This disparity underscores the potential developmental and contextual factors that shape domain-specific characteristics, such as greater impulsivity or emerging cognitive strategies in younger individuals.61,62 However, the smaller contribution of executive function in young adults should not be solely attributed to neurotoxic effects, given the higher severity of alcohol use in comparison with the older sample. Variability in age range also matters: executive function measures correlated strongly with age in our adult sample but weakly in our young adult sample, probably due to restriction of range. Moreover, executive function is multifaceted, with impulsivity declining but cognitive task performance generally improving with age,16,59,63, prior to cognitive decline in later life.64 Finally, SHAP analyses confirmed mixed positive and negative effects across sub-facets, underscoring heterogeneity within this domain. Moreover, the adult sample consistently demonstrated higher R2 values across models, suggesting better predictive power. This difference can be partly due to more developed executive functioning and emotion regulation, which can gradually alter the balance among the three ANA domains. Additionally, the larger sample size in the adult dataset may have contributed to higher R2 values, reflecting greater model stability rather than substantive differences across age groups.65
The ANA domains are inherently multifaceted, involving intercorrelated features and varying scales of measurement, which necessitate a tailored analytical approach.17 Elastic net likely outperformed the other models because of its combined L1 and L2 penalties which encourage both variable selection and coefficient shrinkage. This balance is particularly useful in settings with many correlated predictors, as it reduces model complexity while retaining important signals. While tree-based methods (e.g., random forest, gradient boosting) and SVMs also handle collinearity, elastic net may have been better aligned with the structure and scale of our data, offering a parsimonious model with strong generalizability. This advantage is especially relevant given the correlations among ANA measures and the relatively moderate sample size. The strong performance of elastic net in this study highlights the importance of evaluating diverse algorithms to identify the best fit for the data's structure and research objectives. While elastic net demonstrated effectiveness in handling multicollinearity and high-dimensional data, the utility of other robust algorithms should not be overlooked. Employing a range of methods allows for a more comprehensive understanding of the data and ensures that findings are robust across different modelling techniques.66 This comparative approach is particularly vital for psychological frameworks like the ANA, where the complexity of constructs demands diverse methodologies to uncover nuanced patterns and enhance predictive accuracy. By exploring multiple algorithms, researchers can better account for the intricate relationships within the data and strengthen the validity of their conclusions. Such strategies not only optimize predictive power but also provide deeper insights into the underlying mechanisms of addiction-related constructs.
The study's strengths include leveraging two independent datasets that capture the whole spectrum of alcohol use, not only the problematic end of the spectrum, enhancing generalizability, and employing robust computational methods to validate the ANA framework. The use of feature importance analyses further elucidates the nuanced contributions of variables, enriching the interpretability of machine learning results. Ultimately incorporating domain-specific metrics into screening and diagnostic procedures may facilitate early identification of high-risk individuals and enable personalized treatment planning. Limitations of the study include the restricted ethnic diversity within the sample, which may constrain the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. The relatively low R2 values observed for the executive function domain highlight potential challenges in measurement or the influence of unaccounted variables. The community adult sample is non-clinical and the majority drank below risk thresholds on the AUDIT and as a result may underestimate the roles of negative emotionality and executive function that are putatively more salient in severe AUD. Future priorities include increasing the representation of individuals at the more severe end of the spectrum. The young adult sample also included regular cannabis users, which could confound the interpretation of domain contributions by reflecting polysubstance rather than alcohol-specific effects, particularly in domains assessed with non-alcohol-specific measures. However, this limitation is partially mitigated by the natural high prevalence of concurrent alcohol and cannabis use in young adults, which may enhance the generalizability of the findings to real-world patterns of substance use. Additionally, the datasets included a broad range of measures across the three ANA domains, but not every possible indicator. Furthermore, the study did not incorporate neurobiological data, such as brain activation during reward processing,67 or genetic data, such as polygenic risk scores for ANA constructs,68,69 or which could provide deeper mechanistic insights into the investigated domains. These limitations emphasize the need for more extensive datasets and advanced modelling techniques in future research. To address these gaps, future studies should extend this approach in larger, more diverse samples and integrate neurobiological and genetic measures. Longitudinal designs will also be crucial for evaluating the stability and predictive validity of ANA domains over time, enabling dynamic assessments of addiction trajectories and enhancing the framework's applicability in both research and clinical contexts. One of the ANA framework's primary goals is to parse heterogeneity in AUD and hazardous drinking patterns. By showing differential contributions of incentive salience, negative emotionality, and executive function across two non-treatment-seeking samples, our results highlight domain-specific pathways that may underlie distinct drinking behaviour phenotypes, thereby advancing efforts to address heterogeneity. Future investigations should extend this work by examining how the relative influence of these domains varies across distinct AUD subtypes, providing a more nuanced understanding of the mechanisms driving heterogeneity.
In sum, this study provides novel insights into the computational evaluation of the ANA framework in relation to hazardous drinking, demonstrating the utility of machine learning in identifying key predictive features across age groups. By highlighting the dominant role of incentive salience and the secondary contributions of negative emotionality and executive function, our findings support the multidimensional nature of addiction-related constructs and their varying influence across developmental stages. The application of elastic net underscores the importance of selecting robust models for high-dimensional psychological data, while the use of two independent datasets enhances the generalizability of our results. These findings contribute to a more refined understanding of hazardous drinking patterns and emphasize the potential value of machine learning approaches to understand addiction. While large health systems may not imminently deploy machine learning pipelines for clinical screening, this approach may inform the refinement of assessment tools, identify high-yield features for more efficient screening, and guide stratification of risk in clinical practice. Ultimately, the dimensional characterization of individuals along ANA domains may support more personalized prevention and intervention strategies. Future work should continue expanding this methodology, incorporating neurobiological and genetic data, and leveraging longitudinal designs to refine predictive models and improve risk assessment and treatment approaches.
