Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by inflammatory demyelination and neurodegeneration within the central nervous system.1 As one of the most prevalent neurological disorders affecting young adults, MS typically manifests between the ages of 20 and 40, with nearly 1 million individuals diagnosed in the United States.2 MS presents with a diverse array of symptoms, including physical symptoms such as pain,3 fatigue,4 and spasticity,5 as well as psychological challenges such as anxiety and depression.6 Furthermore, cognitive impairments, particularly deficits in memory function and processing speed, often emerge early in the disease course and progressively worsen over time.7,8 The cumulative burden of these symptoms significantly impacts the quality of life of individuals living with MS.9 Despite advancements in MS research, effective pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatment options for symptom management remain limited.10
Given that clinician-prescribed treatments may not fully manage symptoms, efforts to self-manage symptoms are commonly observed in individuals with MS. Self-management of symptoms may include consumption of psychoactive substances in a manner that is similar to how the general public may consume a caffeine drink to combat fatigue or nicotine to manage stress. Cannabis11–13 and opioids14 are commonly used to alleviate pain, spasticity, and sleep disturbances in people with MS. Alcohol is frequently consumed to reduce stress and enhance mood.15 Caffeine has been shown to have positive effects on mobility and quality of life in MS.16
While some psychoactive substances have shown therapeutic potential in MS — such as cannabis for pain and spasticity relief13 — there is growing concern regarding their potential inadvertent adverse effects. Substance use, such as alcohol and cannabis, has been linked to worsened depression17,18 and accelerated cognitive decline.19,20 Neuroimaging studies in people with substance use disorders have revealed significant neurobiological alterations, including structural and functional abnormalities in the brain.21 Different substances appear to exert distinct cognitive effects. Cannabis use has shown between-person association with poorer cognitive performance20,22,23 and perceived cognitive function,11 whereas caffeine consumption has shown cognitive benefits.24
However, previous research on MS has primarily focused on cannabis and caffeine, leaving gaps in understanding the cognitive impact of other commonly used substances such as alcohol, nicotine, and opioids. Additionally, most studies have relied on cross-sectional designs, which provide insights into the between-person associations between substance use and cognitive function (e.g., people who use cannabis tend to exhibit worse cognitive function compared to those who do not), but fail to elucidate the temporal within-person associations (e.g., when people use substances, do they tend to have worse or better than usual cognitive function immediately or shortly after?). Therefore, the present study aims to investigate the impact of substance use on the subsequent cognitive function in people with MS by employing ecological momentary assessment (EMA) to capture real-time self-reported substance use and subjective cognitive function, and ambulatory cognitive tests to assess real-time cognitive performance. We hypothesize that central nervous system depressants (i.e., alcohol, cannabis, opioids) will be associated with subsequent declines in perceived cognitive function and cognitive performance, whereas stimulants (i.e., caffeine, nicotine) will be associated with improvements in perceived cognitive function and cognitive performance. By examining these relationships, this study seeks to enhance our understanding of the within-person association between cognitive function and substance use in MS, informing future clinical guidelines to support cognitive health in this population.
Methods
Participants
This secondary analysis used microlongitudinal data from a 14-day study on cognitive changes in MS.25 To participate, individuals had to meet the following criteria: age ≥18, fluent English, confirmed MS diagnosis, and ability to walk ≥50% of the time (with or without aid). Exclusion criteria included MS relapse in the past 30 days or inability to use study tools.
Data collection
Following approval from the University of Michigan’s medical institutional review board, participants were recruited from three research sites: the University of Michigan (Ann Arbor, MI), Wayne State University (Detroit, MI), and the University of Washington (Seattle, WA). Recruitment occurred across three U.S. research sites via registries, clinical referrals, and outreach. After phone screening, eligible individuals completed a baseline lab visit (up to 2.5 hours) for informed consent and baseline assessment, followed by 14 days of EMA and cognitive testing at four time points daily (morning, midday, evening, and bedtime) using a study-provided smartphone. Data were collected from May 2022 to April 2024. This manuscript describes the initial data collection (first laboratory visit and 14-day ambulatory monitoring period).
Measures
Laboratory visit measures
Participants completed a self-reported survey of demographics such as age, sex, race, education, and employment. In addition, clinical characteristics include MS subtype, years since diagnosis, and self-reported disability (Patient Determined Disease Steps [PDDS]).26
Ambulatory Measures
Substance use was assessed by asking “Since the last assessment, did you consume (check all that apply): 1) alcohol; 2) caffeine; 3) nicotine; 4) cannabis; 5) opioids; 6) none of the above?” Perceived cognitive function27 was assessed using three visual analog scale items (0–100), with higher scores indicating greater cognitive difficulties (total score range: 0–300). For example, participants rated, “RIGHT NOW…How foggy is your thinking?” (Figure 1). Higher scores indicate worse perceived cognitive function. For objective cognitive function measures, the Dot Memory Test and the Symbol Search Test were administered to assess processing speed and working memory, respectively.28 For the Dot Memory Test, each trial consisted of three phases: (1) encoding, where participants viewed and memorized the location of three red dots on a 5×5 grid for 3 seconds; (2) distraction, where they completed a visual search task (identifying F’s among E’s); and (3) retrieval, where they placed the red dots on a blank grid in the remembered locations. Accuracy and reaction time were recorded, and only sessions with ≥70% accuracy were included. For the Symbol Search Test, participants viewed four symbol pairs at the top of the screen and two symbol pairs at the bottom. They selected the pair at the bottom that matched one of the top pairs as quickly as possible. Each task was administered four times daily. Both measures have demonstrated strong psychometric properties across diverse populations, including people with MS28,29, and showed excellent reliability in the current sample, with intraclass correlation coefficients of 0.99 across all cognitive tasks.
Data analyses
Participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics (i.e., means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages). Given the nested structure of the data, we employed mixed-effects multilevel modeling (MLM) to examine the temporal within-person association of psychoactive substance use (alcohol, nicotine, caffeine, opioids, and cannabis) with cognitive function. Each substance use variable was dummy coded (0 = no, 1 = yes). In addition, the total number of each substance used per participant was included in the models to capture between-person differences in substance use. After controlling for age, sex, education years, and PDDS, three separate MLM analyses were conducted, each corresponding to a different cognitive measure, to determine whether psychoactive substance use predicts perceived cognitive function, working memory (mean error distance), and processing speed (median response time). In these models, both substance use and cognitive function were assessed at the same timepoint; however, the substance use questionnaire assessed use since the previous evaluation. Therefore, the substance use variable temporally preceded the cognitive outcome, allowing for a naturally lagged interpretation of within-person associations without the need for explicit time-lagged variable construction. To account for potential confounding by time of day (e.g., variations in substance use or cognitive performance across different timepoints), both day and four EMA timepoints were included as covariates in all models. Random intercepts were incorporated to account for individual variability. A first-order autoregressive covariance structure was selected a priori based on the expectation of temporally declining correlation as the data was collected over time, and confirmed as the best-fitting structure compared to alternative specifications using AIC model fit indices. Model parameters were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood. Missing data were retained, as MLM is robust to missing values.30 All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0. A power analysis was performed using the GLIMMPSE software,31 with a sample size of 257 and an alpha level of 0.05, which indicated that the study had greater than 80% power to detect statistically significant within-person effects.
Results
Participants characteristics
Table 1 provides a summary of sample characteristics. Of 274 enrolled people with MS, 257 completed the home monitoring phase. The mean age of participants was 50.9±11.8 years, and 77.4% were female. Most of the participants were White (73.1%) and had relapsing-remitting (83.2%), with an average disease duration of 16.4 years. Across the study, participants could complete up to 56 assessments each, totaling 14,392 possible data points. Completion rates were high: 77.6% for substance use, 77.2% for perceived cognition, 76.5% for the Dot Memory Test, and 76.1% for the Symbol Search Test. Caffeine was the most commonly used substance (83.7%), followed by alcohol (49.4%), cannabis (28.8%), nicotine (14.4%), and opioids (8.2%). Among users, the median frequency of reported use (as a percentage of assessments) was highest for nicotine (50.0%) and most variable across individuals.
Between-person association between psychoactive substance use and cognitive function
MLM analyses indicated that, at the between-person level, there were no significant associations between psychoactive substance use and cognitive function (Table 2). Individuals who reported higher average use of substances across the monitoring period did not differ in either perceived or objective cognitive performance compared to those who used less.
Within-person temporal association between psychoactive substance use and cognitive function
Table 2 presents the within-person associations between psychoactive substance use and later cognitive function. Caffeine use was associated with better perceived cognitive performance; participants reported fewer cognitive difficulties following caffeine consumption (B = −3.31, p < .01). Follow-up analyses indicated that this association held across all three items assessing perceived cognitive function. In contrast, cannabis use was associated with increased self-reported cognitive difficulties (B = 3.38, p < .05), particularly with reports of slowed thinking (B = 1.66, p = .02). Alcohol, nicotine, and opioid use were not significantly associated with perceived cognitive function. Caffeine use was also linked to improved processing speed, reflected by faster response times on the Symbol Search Test (B = −32.58, p = .01). A post-hoc analysis found a significant interaction between momentary caffeine use and individuals’ average caffeine use (B = −3.12, p = .04), indicating that the beneficial effect of caffeine on processing speed was more pronounced among individuals with lower average caffeine consumption. Alcohol use predicted poorer working memory performance, as indicated by greater error distance on the Dot Memory Test (B = 0.05, p = .02). No significant associations were observed for nicotine, cannabis, or opioid use with objective cognitive function.
Discussion
This study investigated the temporal association between psychoactive substance use and cognitive function in people with MS. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine within-person relationships across multiple substances in this population. Our findings indicate that caffeine and alcohol were the most commonly used substances, followed by cannabis, while nicotine and opioid use were infrequent. Notably, caffeine consumption showed a within-person association with better perceived cognitive function and faster processing speed, although it did not significantly affect working memory. In contrast, alcohol use was associated with subsequent declines in working memory, and cannabis use was linked to increased perceived cognitive difficulties. Nicotine and opioid use showed no significant within-person temporal associations with cognitive function.
While no substances demonstrated significant between-person associations with cognitive function in our study, prior research investigating between-person associations suggests that long-term substance use can have cognitive effects. For instance, prolonged cannabis use affects multiple cognitive domains such as processing speed, working memory, and executive function, among people with MS compared to non-users.20 Similarly, chronic alcohol and opioid use are associated with cognitive impairment in the general population.32,33 Conversely, moderate caffeine intake has been linked to better verbal memory performance in people with MS.34 The discrepancy between our findings and previous studies may stem from differences in study design, particularly our short-term EMA approach (14 days), which may not have been sufficient to capture between-person effects. Additionally, we did not assess the duration or dose of substance use, limiting our ability to evaluate its cumulative impact on cognition. It is also important to note that this study did not recruit participants based on their substance use patterns, so our sample likely included a limited number of individuals with habitual or heavy substance use, further reducing our power to detect between-person effects. Future studies that recruit individuals with varying levels of substance use and incorporate detailed substance use characterization will be essential to better understand the cognitive consequences of long-term psychoactive substance use in MS. The significant within-person effects observed for caffeine, alcohol, and cannabis suggest that these substances exert temporary, state-dependent effects on cognitive function in people with MS. Caffeine, a well-established central nervous system stimulant, enhances cognitive performance by blocking adenosine receptors and increasing dopamine and norepinephrine release.35 In healthy populations, caffeine improves alertness and attention,36 and similar effects may extend to individuals with MS. The significant interaction between within- and between-person levels of use suggests that non-habitual caffeine users may experience greater cognitive benefits from caffeine intake, potentially due to increased sensitivity to its stimulant effects. However, the lack of a significant effect on working memory suggests that caffeine’s benefits may be more pronounced in attention-related domains rather than working memory. Future studies exploring various cognitive domains could further clarify caffeine’s role in MS-related cognitive function.
There is limited research on the effects of alcohol consumption on cognitive function in people with MS to compare the findings from this study. However, extensive literature in the general population indicates that excessive alcohol consumption can impair cognitive function.37,38 Alcohol has been shown to disrupt immediate memory and information retrieval,39 which aligns with our finding that alcohol consumption was associated with transient declines in working memory. While occasional alcohol use may have minimal long-term consequences, chronic and excessive alcohol consumption has been related to persistent memory deficits32,40 and structural brain damage, particularly in the hippocampus, hypothalamus, and cerebellum—regions crucial for memory.41,42 These neurotoxic effects may accelerate cognitive decline, particularly in individuals already vulnerable to cognitive impairment, such as those with MS. Given these risks, healthcare providers should assess drinking habits in this population and provide targeted interventions to mitigate potential long-term effects. Notably, research suggests that cognitive dysfunction related to alcohol use can improve with sustained abstinence of over a year.43 Individuals with MS who engage in chronic alcohol use may benefit from tailored guidance that considers clinically meaningful patterns of alcohol consumption. Similar approaches have been proposed in other neurological populations to help reduce alcohol use.44
Our study found that cannabis use in people with MS is associated with increased perceived cognitive difficulties but showed no significant relationship with objective cognitive measures. Cannabis exerts its effects through the activation of cannabinoid receptors (CB1 and CB2) in the central nervous system, which modulates neurotransmitter release and has been linked to cognitive impairment.45,46 However, the cognitive effects of cannabis appear to be influenced by tolerance, as frequent users develop resistance to its impact on cognitive function, while occasional users experience greater impairments.47,48 This may explain the lack of association with objective cognitive measures in our study, given that more than half of cannabis users (52.7%) reported frequent use (≥10 times) during the data collection period. Another possible explanation for our findings lies in the specific composition of cannabis used by participants. Cannabis products vary widely in their concentrations of delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and cannabidiol (CBD), the two primary active cannabinoids. Previous research has shown that high-THC cannabis (e.g., Dronabinol) is associated with memory impairment, whereas cannabis with higher CBD content (e.g., Nabiximol) does not produce significant cognitive deficits.49,50 Nabiximols, a cannabis-based medication approved for treating spasticity in MS, has been demonstrated to have no detrimental effects on cognitive function in this population.51 While CBD has been found to mitigate the cognitive impairments associated with THC, it does not reduce psychotomimetic effects, as both high- and low-CBD cannabis have been associated with elevated symptoms of cognitive distortion.49 This may help explain our finding that cannabis use was associated only with greater perceived cognitive difficulties. Future research should consider not only the frequency and quantity of cannabis use but also the chemical composition of cannabinoid products consumed to better understand their impact on cognition in MS.
Nicotine and opioid use showed no significant within-person temporal associations with cognitive function. Nicotine has been shown to enhance attention and working memory in the short term by stimulating nicotinic acetylcholine receptors,52,53 yet long-term use has been associated with a twofold increase in dementia risk in older adults.54 The lack of association in our study may be due to the low prevalence of nicotine use or potential disease-related differences in neurophysiological responses in MS. Similarly, while chronic opioid use is known to impair cognitive function, particularly in memory and attention,33,55 the infrequent use reported in our sample may have limited our ability to detect significant effects. Given the potential cognitive risks associated with chronic nicotine and opioid use, future studies should investigate their long-term impact in MS, taking into account dosage, duration, and disease progression. A clearer understanding of these relationships will be essential for developing clinical guidelines on substance use in people with MS.
Limitations
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while this study examined temporal associations, causal conclusions cannot be drawn due to the observational design. Additionally, our findings are based on a 14-day EMA approach, which may not fully capture the long-term cognitive effects of substance use, particularly habitual substance use or substance abuse. Future research employing experimental and longitudinal designs is needed to provide more robust evidence. Second, the study relied on self-reported substance use, which is subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Participants may have underreported or misreported their substance use, particularly for substances like cannabis, due to stigma or forgetfulness. The EMA items used to assess substance use were developed specifically for this study, and their psychometric properties have not yet been evaluated. Furthermore, the approach could not capture important contextual features such as dose, substance formulation (e.g., THC vs CBD), route of administration, or proximity of use to the time of cognitive assessment (e.g., use one hour vs three hours prior). In addition, although our EMA design captured whether a substance was used within a given interval, it could not assess additive use throughout the day, nor the chronicity of use over time. These limitations constrain interpretation of the observed associations, which should be considered preliminary. Third, while the EMA items for perceived cognitive function have been used in adult populations,27,56 they have not been validated in people with MS. However, in the current sample, this measure demonstrated excellent reliability. Fourth, although both day and EMA timepoint were statistically controlled for in the analyses, we cannot fully rule out residual confounding due to clustering of certain substances at specific times of day. For example, caffeine use may be concentrated in the morning, when cognitive performance also tends to be better. Additionally, substance use reported at the morning assessment may reflect behaviors from the prior evening, creating a longer lag between exposure and cognitive outcome compared to other timepoints. This could introduce inconsistency in temporal alignment across the day. However, we did not find significant interaction between EMA timepoint and substance use in predicting cognitive outcomes, which provides some reassurance that time-of-day confounding may not fully account for the observed effects. Future studies may consider redesigning morning prompts to separately assess overnight versus early morning use to increase temporal precision. Furthermore, although participants were asked to report use “since the last assessment,” the system did not display the exact time or type of the prior assessment. In cases of intermittent missingness (e.g., missing midday but completing the evening prompt), participants may have interpreted the recall window inconsistently, potentially introducing additional bias into the substance use data. Future work should examine whether missingness patterns affect the associations observed. Lastly, the low prevalence of nicotine and opioid use in our sample limited our statistical power to detect significant associations. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples, as well as more comprehensive and validated substance use measures, are needed to clarify the nature and extent of these relationships. In addition, while statistically significant, the clinical meaningfulness of the observed effects remains unclear due to the absence of established minimal clinically important differences for EMA-based cognitive measures. Addressing this gap represents an important direction for future ambulatory research.
Conclusions
This study provides valuable insights into the within-person temporal associations between psychoactive substance use and cognitive function in people with MS using EMA. Our findings showed that specific substance use is temporally related to later time point cognitive function. These findings highlight the importance of developing personalized, evidence-based guidance for substance use in people with MS, tailored to individual cognitive profiles and symptom trajectories. To inform robust clinical recommendations, future studies should integrate longer monitoring periods, objective biomarkers of substance exposure, and detailed analyses of substance composition and use patterns. These efforts will be critical for informing safe substance use strategies that optimize both symptom management and cognitive outcomes in people living with MS.
