Introduction
Evidence that a longer duration of untreated psychosis is associated with poorer clinical outcomes (1) has motivated efforts to identify psychosis during its prodrome, typically a 1-2 year period characterized by attenuated psychosis symptoms and a decline in functioning, with the goal of improving the illness course or even preventing its onset. While clinical criteria identifying prodromal symptoms have been validated, only approximately 20% of individuals at clinical high risk for psychosis (CHR) convert to a psychotic disorder within two years (2). This low conversion rate limits the justification for using antipsychotic medications to treat individuals with CHR symptoms, creating a major research imperative to identify biomarkers that improve prediction of clinical outcomes. One promising strategy for identifying biomarkers that predate and predict psychosis onset in CHR individuals involves consideration of neurophysiological measures known to be abnormal in schizophrenia (3). In particular, electroencephalography (EEG)-based event-related potential (ERP) components have gained traction in the study of CHR and its clinical outcomes (4, 5).
P300 amplitude reduction and latency delay are among the most widely replicated of the ERP component abnormalities identified in schizophrenia (6-8), rendering P300 a leading candidate biomarker for predicting psychosis risk in CHR individuals. P300 is a positive voltage deflection in the stimulus-locked ERP occurring approximately 300ms after stimulus onset. It is typically elicited during an oddball target detection task by infrequently presented salient stimuli interspersed among frequent “standard” stimuli (9). P300 amplitude is thought to reflect phasic attentional shifting (10), attentional resource allocation (11-13), working memory updating of stimulus context (14, 15), stimulus salience (16, 17), and/or stimulus expectancy (18, 19), whereas P300 latency is thought to reflect stimulus processing speed(20, 21).
There are two subcomponents of P300 that depend on the eliciting stimulus (9). P3b reflects an effortful “top-down” attentional shift to an infrequent target stimulus that requires a response when presented. P3a peaks 20-50ms earlier than P3b and is elicited by infrequent novel non-target stimuli, reflecting automatic “bottom-up” attentional orienting to salient distractors during oddball task performance. Target P3b and novelty P3a amplitudes are reduced in schizophrenia when presented during auditory (22-38) and visual (22, 35, 39-41) tasks. Auditory P300 has generally shown trait-like reductions in schizophrenia (24, 42) but also appears to be somewhat sensitive to clinical state fluctuations (24), and abnormalities worsen with longer illness duration (43, 44). Visual P300 may also be sensitive to clinical state (45), given evidence that amplitudes increase with clinical improvement (46). In general, auditory P300 abnormalities are larger and more robust in schizophrenia than visual P300 abnormalities (6, 7).
Several studies have also shown that auditory target P3b (22, 30, 47-55) and novelty P3a (22, 28-30, 47, 56, 57) are reduced in the CHR syndrome and that auditory target P3b, in particular, is sensitive to future clinical outcomes among longitudinally tracked individuals (22, 47, 53, 58) but see also (59). Among the largest of these studies is the North American Prodrome Longitudinal Study (NAPLS-2), which showed reduced auditory target P3b amplitude in CHR individuals at baseline to predict shorter time to psychosis conversion and more intact amplitude to predict future remission from the CHR syndrome (47). Despite evidence of visual P300 deficits in schizophrenia, however, relatively few studies have examined visual P300 among CHR individuals, finding reductions in visual target P3b (22, 60, 61) and novelty P3a (22, 62). In the only study that examined visual P300 and included longitudinal follow-ups to ascertain clinical outcomes in a small sample of CHR individuals, P300 was associated with future conversion to psychosis and shorter time to conversion across auditory and visual modalities, suggesting that P300 elicited in both modalities may predict future outcomes at the CHR stage (22).
Here, we present results from the largest study to date to examine P300 recorded during a visual oddball task, completed by CHR individuals as part of NAPLS-2. In addition to examining baseline differences between CHR and healthy comparison (HC) groups, we evaluated whether visual P300 amplitudes were associated with future clinical outcomes. Based on prior auditory oddball studies, we hypothesized that CHR individuals would have reduced visual P300 amplitudes, and that target P3b amplitude, in particular, would predict clinical outcomes after two years of follow-up.
Methods and Materials
Participants
The eight-site NAPLS-2 followed 764 outpatients meeting CHR criteria and 269 demographically similar HC participants ages 12-35 (63). Of these, 538 CHR and 230 HC participants provided usable electroencephalography (EEG) data collected during a visual oddball task at baseline (Figure S1). CHR participants met the Criteria of Psychosis-Risk Syndromes (COPS) based on the Structured Interview for Psychosis-Risk Syndromes (SIPS) (64, 65), and symptoms were rated using the Scale of Psychosis-Risk Symptoms (SOPS). Exclusion criteria included: current/lifetime psychotic disorder, IQ below 70, or a central nervous system disorder. HCs could not have a first-degree relative with a psychotic disorder and could not currently be taking antipsychotic medications. Details of recruitment and inclusion/exclusion criteria have been described previously (63). Adult participants provided written informed consent, and minors provided written assent with parents providing written informed consent.
Participants completed baseline EEG recording and were followed over a 24-month period or until they transitioned to psychosis, undergoing clinical assessments every 6 months. Of the individuals with visual oddball data, 71 converted to psychosis and met Presence of Psychotic Symptoms (65) criteria (CHR-Converters), and 218 completed 24-month clinical follow-up but did not convert (CHR-Nonconverters). CHR-Nonconverters were also further classified by their clinical outcomes at the 24-month assessment (63) as either symptom remission (CHR-Remitters, n=86; no longer meeting COPS criteria) or syndrome progression and/or symptom persistence (CHR-Persistent, n=132; continuing to meet COPS criteria or experiencing attenuated positive symptoms over the previous 4 weeks). Baseline clinical assessment was completed on a separate day from electroencephalography recording (median [SD] time between assessments, 24.00 [56.17] days).
Of the 538 CHR participants, 122 (22.7%) were taking antipsychotic medications at baseline but were included because clinical history indicated that medication was initiated when symptoms were in the CHR range and that the participant had never experienced full psychosis. This medicated CHR subgroup included 22 of 71 CHR converters (31.0%) and 42 of the 218 CHR nonconverters (19.3%).
Oddball Paradigm
The visual oddball task, presented with Presentation Software (Neurobehavioral Systems, Berkeley, CA), comprised three blocks of 450 stimuli, which included a pseudorandom sequence of frequently presented “standard” stimuli (80%), infrequent “target” stimuli (10%), and infrequent unique “novel” distractor stimuli (10%). Standards were a small blue circle and targets were a large blue circle presented at the vertical and horizontal meridian with the radius of targets to standards having a ratio of 104:67. Stimuli were presented for 500ms, and the stimulus onset asynchrony between trials was jittered 1500-2500ms (mean=2000ms) to avoid simultaneous presentation with auditory stimuli from a background mismatch negativity task (66). To prevent consecutive presentations of target and/or novel stimuli, each was always followed by at least one standard stimulus. Participants viewed an instructional cartoon and heard recorded instructions to press a response key to targets using their preferred hand while withholding responses to standard and novel stimuli. Trials with incorrect button presses were excluded from analysis. Following exclusion of 2 participants (1 HC, 1 CHR-Nonconverter) for poor performance (≤50% accuracy), response accuracy was 99.0% in both CHR and HC groups.
Electroencephalographic Data Acquisition and Preprocessing
Participants sat at a computer monitor (resolution=1,024×768; refresh rate=60Hz). EEG was recorded at 1024Hz using a 32-channel (4 sites) or 64-channel (4 sites) BioSemi ActiveTwo recording system (BioSemi, Amsterdam, Netherlands). Additional electrodes were placed on the nose and mastoids with electrooculogram recorded at electrodes above and below the right eye (vertical: VEOG) and at the outer canthus of each eye (horizontal; HEOG).
Continuous EEG data were re-referenced to averaged mastoids and high-pass filtered (0.1Hz). Data were segmented into epochs from −1000-2000ms time-locked to stimulus onset and processed using a modified version (see 47) of the Fully Automated Statistical Thresholding for EEG artifact Rejection (FASTER) Routine (67), with additional modification of ICA component selection (68) to ensure removal of visual artifacts where blinks and saccades may be temporally correlated with ERP components. After averaging epochs to form stimulus-specific ERP waves, a low-pass filter (15Hz) was applied (69) followed by baseline correction (−100-0ms).
P300 amplitude was disambiguated by computing difference waves that subtracted the standard ERP from target (P3b) and novel (P3a) ERPs and was defined as the most positive peak between 230–600ms, as in prior studies (22, 42). Peak amplitudes were identified at electrode Cz for P3a and at Pz for P3b, where these components show their scalp maxima. P3a and P3b amplitudes were calculated as the average value in a 30ms window centered on the peaks identified at Cz and Pz, respectively, while latencies were defined as times at which the peaks occurred.
Statistical Analysis
Analyses were conducted on age- and study site-adjusted P300 amplitude, latency, and median reaction time (RT) z-scores generated using a linear mixed model in all participants, with site modeled as a random effect and Group (HC, CHR), Age, and Group x Age modeled as fixed effects. Using the best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP) adjustments for random site effects based on all participants, and the partial regression coefficients for the age-regression line slope and intercept estimated for HC, predicted scores were generated representing the value expected for a healthy individual of their age plus the BLUP adjustment for their site. The standard error of regression (SER) was estimated in the HC as: SERHC=sqrt[∑(Observed−Predicted)2∕(NHC−P−1)], where NHC=HC sample size and P=number of fixed effect parameters estimated for HC (in this case, 1 for age). Age- and site-adjusted z-scores were then calculated for each participant as: z=(Observed score−Predicted score based on individual’s age and site)∕SERHC
While this is similar to our previously described adjustment procedure (22, 47, 66, 70-72), it models site as a random effect based on all participants rather than as a fixed effect in HC, preventing potential over-adjustment for random site differences that can occur when treated as fixed, and injection of HC differences between sites into site-adjusted CHR scores. Resulting age- and site-adjusted z-scores reflect the degree to which a participant’s values, in standard deviation units, differ from the value expected for their specific age and from someone from their site. This approach removes normal aging and attenuates site-specific effects while retaining potential pathological aging effects (e.g., abnormal brain maturation trajectories) in the CHR data (Table S1 shows P300 correlations with age).
Group differences in target P3b and novelty P3a amplitude and latency z-scores, as well as z-scores of median target RT, were tested using analysis of variance models, with two groups (CHR, HC), three groups (CHR-Converter, CHR-Nonconverter, HC), or four groups (CHR-Converter, CHR-Persistent, CHR-Remitter, HC) as the between-subjects factor, and for ERP analyses, stimulus type (novel, target) as the within-subjects factor. Additional models tested group differences separately for each stimulus type. The false discovery rate procedure (73) was used to account for multiple comparisons (adjusted p-values are reported). Group effect sizes are reported as Cohen’s d. All models were rerun excluding CHR individuals taking antipsychotic medication at baseline, and also excluding individuals taking antidepressant, benzodiazepine, and mood stabilizing medications at baseline. To assess associations between positive and negative symptom severity and P300, novelty P3a and target P3b amplitude and latency z-scores were correlated with SOPS symptom scores.
Cox regression was performed to model the association of P300 amplitudes, latencies, and target RT z-scores with time to psychosis onset among all CHR participants, with censoring when participants were lost-to-follow-up or did not convert after their last follow-up assessment. In addition, P300 z-scores for targets and novels were included in a single model to evaluate independent contributions of each to predicting time to psychosis onset. An additional Cox regression tested the association between P300 and time to conversion over and above positive symptoms by first entering modified SOPS P1 (unusual thought content/delusional ideas) + P2 (suspiciousness/persecutory ideas) combination scores that were implemented in the NAPLS-2 risk calculator (74) and previously shown to be the strongest clinical predictor of psychosis onset (75, 76).
Alpha was p=.05 with 2-tailed testing.
Results
Sample Characteristics
Sample characteristics are reported in Table 1. Sex and age did not differ by group.
P300 Amplitude
Grand average ERP deviant-standard difference waves, scalp topographic maps, and mean amplitude values for target and novel stimuli are shown in Figure 1 (Tables S2-S3 show P300 amplitude and latency by group and Figures S2 and S3 show target, novel, and standard grand average ERP waveforms). P300 amplitude z-scores, averaged across target (P3b) and novel (P3a) stimulus types, differed by group [F(1,766)=4.76; p=.029] such that CHR participants had reduced amplitudes compared to HCs (d=.14). This group effect did not interact with stimulus type [F(1,766)=0.27; p=.61].
Grand average ERP difference waves, scalp topographic maps, and mean amplitude values by 24-month conversion status group relative to HC are shown in Figure 2. Testing the three-group model, baseline P300 amplitude z-scores, averaged across target (P3b) and novel (P3a) stimuli, differed by group [F(2,516)=5.66; p=.004], such that CHR-Converters had significantly reduced P300 amplitudes compared to both CHR-Nonconverters (p=.02, d=.28) and HC (p<.003, d=.39). However, CHR-Nonconverters were not reduced relative to HC (p=.12, d=.10). The group effect did not interact with stimulus type [F(2,516)=0.17, p=.84].
Although our a priori hypothesis that clinical outcome group differences would be significantly greater for target P3b than for novel P3a was not supported, we separately tested group effects by stimulus type to facilitate comparisons with prior studies (4, 5, 22, 47, 59). Target P3b amplitude z-scores differed by group [F(2,516)=3.96, p=.020). CHR-Converters had significantly reduced target P3b amplitudes relative to CHR-Nonconverters (p=.036, d=.32) and HCs (p=.015, d=.41), whereas CHR-Nonconverters and HC did not significantly differ (p=.45, d=.07). Novelty P3a amplitude z-scores also differed by group [F(2,516)=3.78, p=.023], but while CHR-Converters had significantly reduced P3a amplitude z-scores relative to HC (p=.02, d=.37), they did not differ from CHR-Nonconverters (p=.12, d=.24). Similarly, CHR-Nonconverters did not differ from HCs (p=.17, d=.13).
The four-group analysis that included CHR-Converters, HC, and CHR-Nonconverters further parsed into CHR-Persistent and CHR-Remitter subgroups revealed no additional information beyond the three-group model (Supplementary Results, Figure S4). In addition, a nearly identical pattern of results was observed when analyses were repeated in the subsamples of participants not taking psychotropic medications (Supplementary Results).
P300 Latency
For P300 latency z-scores across target (P3b) and novel (P3a) stimulus types, the two-group (CHR, HC) model did not show a significant effect of group [F(1,766)=0.31; p=.58] or a significant group x stimulus type interaction [F(1,766=0.005; p=.94]. In the three-group model, there was a marginally significant group effect [F(2,516=22.99; p=.051] but no significant group x stimulus type interaction [F(2,516=0.65; p=.52]. Post-hoc tests of pairwise group differences on average P300 latency across stimulus types were not statistically significant after FDR correction (ps>.07). In the four-group model, neither the main effect of group [F(2,515)=2.03, p=.11], nor the group x stimulus type interaction [F(3,515=0.81; p=.49], was significant.
Reaction Time
Median target RT did not show significant group effects in the two-group model [F(1,766)=0.20; p=.66], the three-group model [F(2,516)=1.96, p=.14], or the four-group model [F(3,515)=1.19; p=.31] (Table S4 shows RTs by group).
Symptom Correlations
There were no significant correlations between target P3b or novelty P3a amplitude z-scores and positive or negative SOPS symptoms in the CHR group (all ps>.30).
Cox Regression Models
Target P3b amplitude z-scores were associated with time from ERP assessment to psychosis conversion among CHR individuals [χ2=5.27; Wald(1)=5.41; p=.02; Exp(B)=0.74; 95% CI, 0.58-0.95], indicating that more deficient P3b amplitude was associated with more imminent risk of conversion. In contrast, novelty P3a amplitude z-scores were not associated with time to conversion [χ2=2.12; Wald(1)=2.12; p=.15; Exp(B)=0.84; 95% CI, 0.66–1.06]. With both target P3b and novelty P3a in the model, target P3b was a significant predictor over and above novelty P3a (overall model: χ2=5.62, p=.06; target P3b: χ2 change=4.34, Wald(1)=4.26, p=.039, Exp(B)=0.72, 95% CI, 0.53-0.98; novelty P3a: χ2 change= 0.25, Wald(1)=0.25, p=.62, Exp(B)=0.92, 95% CI, 0.68-1.26). Controlling for P1 + P2 symptoms, target P3b amplitude remained a significant predictor of time to conversion (overall model: χ2=22.12; p<.001; target P3b: χ2 change=4.96, Wald(1)=4.78, p=.029, Exp(B)=0.76, 95% CI, 0.59–0.97; P1 + P2 symptoms model: χ2=17.46, Wald(1)=17.06, p<.001, Exp(B)=1.37, 95% CI, 1.18–1.60]. In addition, there was a trend toward slower (i.e., more deficient) median target RT z-scores being associated with shorter time to conversion (overall model: χ2=3.66, Wald(1)=3.67, p=.056, Exp(B)=1.21, 95% CI, 1.00-1.48). All Cox regression results remained unchanged when restricted to the unmedicated CHR subsample (Supplementary Results). Discussion
In the largest study of visual P300 among at-risk individuals to date, we examined whether P300 amplitude is deficient in the CHR syndrome and predicted future clinical outcomes after two years. Consistent with prior studies on visual P300 (22, 60-62), we provided further evidence of amplitude reductions in CHR individuals. In addition, we found that baseline visual P300 amplitudes are reduced among future CHR-Converters relative to CHR-Nonconverters when averaged across both target (P3b) and novel (P3a) stimuli. Although this association with conversion did not significantly depend on stimulus type, it was primarily driven by the relatively greater reduction in visual target P3b amplitude in future CHR-Converters, as, when examined separately from target P3b, the difference in visual novelty P3a amplitude between CHR-Converters and CHR-Nonconverters did not reach statistical significance. Moreover, we found that smaller visual target P3b amplitudes, in particular, were significantly associated with shorter time to psychosis conversion among CHR participants over and above the clinical symptoms (74) most predictive of future conversion. Although the predictive relationship of target P3b amplitudes was modest relative to that of clinical symptoms, these results suggest that the prognostic information provided by visual target P3b is not redundant with the strongest symptom predictors of psychosis onset, highlighting the potential of visual target P3b to augment clinical information in the service of individualized risk stratification, increasing the precision of psychosis conversion risk estimates.
Results are consistent with a prior longitudinal study based on a smaller CHR sample that found more deficient target P3b amplitudes to be associated with conversion to psychosis and to predict shorter time to psychosis onset, irrespective of whether P3b was elicited by auditory or visual targets (22). In the present study, we also observed that more deficient visual target P3b amplitudes, but not novelty P3a amplitudes, predicted shorter time to psychosis onset. This suggests that visual target P3b amplitude may be a useful biomarker of the imminence of psychosis risk that improves prediction of CHR clinical outcomes beyond the accuracy afforded by baseline clinical symptoms alone.
Using auditory oddball data from the same NAPLS-2 sample, we previously showed that future CHR-Remitters had essentially normal auditory target P3b amplitudes at baseline, whereas P3b amplitude deficiencies were similarly evident in CHR-Persistent and CHR-Converter groups (47). Here, we did not find this same association with future remission; visual target P3b amplitudes were similar in the CHR-Remitter and CHR-Persistent groups and fell intermediately between HC and the significantly deficient CHR-Converters. Moreover, while we previously found that target reaction times during the auditory oddball task were longer among future CHR-Converters and predictive of earlier psychosis onset, reaction times did not differentiate CHR clinical outcome groups in the visual task (47), although the Cox regression analysis did show a trend association between longer reaction times and earlier conversion. Together, these results suggest that auditory and visual target P300s and reaction times may play unique roles in differentiating among future clinical outcomes in CHR individuals. The findings that target P3b is more strongly associated with future conversion than novelty P3a also implicate more disruption of temporoparietal-mediated “top-down” attention allocation processes, reflected by deficient P3b to task-relevant target stimuli, relative to the “bottom-up” attention orienting processes subserved by frontal systems that respond to novel non-target stimuli reflected by P3a (9, 77). P3a amplitude, which is reduced in CHR but less sensitive to psychosis conversion, could reflect vulnerability for psychosis, with the onset of full psychosis being dependent on other factors interacting with this vulnerability.
These results may also provide insights into pathophysiological processes involved in psychosis onset. Neuroanatomically, depth electrode and lesion studies have localized P3a to prefrontal cortical regions, while P3b has been linked primarily to temporal-parietal areas (78-80); associations between target P3b amplitude deficits and conversion potentially implicate compromise of these regions in CHR individuals at greatest risk. Neurochemically, target P3b and novelty P3a depend on n-methyl-d-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) neurotransmission, evidenced by pharmacological challenge studies demonstrating that the NMDAR antagonist ketamine reduces their amplitudes in healthy individuals (81). Thus, visual P300 deficits, particularly among future CHR-Converters, are consistent with hypothesized NMDAR hypofunction as an underlying pathophysiological mechanism in schizophrenia (82, 83). However, P300 has also been linked to functioning of dopaminergic (9), noradrenergic (84), and GABAergic systems (85, 86) and serotonin 5-HT2A (87), cholinergic muscarinic (86), and cannabinoid (88, 89) receptors. Finally, animal studies have identified P300-like ERP components in monkeys, who show modulation of P300 amplitude by target tone probability during an oddball target detection task, as in humans (90). Translational bridges offered by ERP measures like P300 motivate ongoing research to refine these measures and further establish homologies with similar measures in animal models to clarify mechanisms involved in psychosis development and establish novel treatment targets.
We did not find latency differences between groups. Prior studies have suggested that P300 latency is delayed in schizophrenia and worsens with illness duration (43, 44). However, prior studies have generally not found P300 latency prolongation in CHR samples (49, 54, 62), consistent with reports that delays emerge later in the course of schizophrenia (43, 44). Given that visual P300 amplitude and latency may differ in their sensitivity to pathological neurodevelopment and progressive changes that emerge with illness chronicity, they likely reflect distinct pathophysiological processes.
Limitations
Although analyses were based on the large NAPLS-2 sample, almost half of the participants did not complete two-year clinical follow-up, preventing their inclusion as CHR-Nonconverters in the analyses comparing clinical outcome groups. However, the full sample was included in the Cox regression analyses, with censoring of data at the time of study withdrawal or when participants were lost to follow-up. While our interpretation of P3b deficits as a predictor of psychosis conversion implies that P3b amplitude captures trait-like vulnerability for psychosis, we cannot rule out state-like deficits that increase in prominence as the individual gets closer to the threshold for conversion or that worsen over time the longer CHR symptoms have been present. Deciphering among these possible interpretations will require future studies to not only retrospectively assess the onset and duration of each CHR-level symptom, but also to assess ERPs at more time points prior to conversion to capture both trait-like deficits but also potential dynamic changes in P3b as symptoms worsen or conversion approaches. Moreover, the presence of antipsychotic and other psychotropic medications was not exclusionary at the time of enrollment, provided there was no evidence that these medications were initiated to treat full psychosis. This potential medication confound was ruled out by multiple analyses in the unmedicated CHR subsamples showing similar results as those obtained in the full sample.
Conclusions
Results from the present study suggest that visual P300 amplitude, primarily P3b elicited by target stimuli, may serve as a prognostic biomarker of future psychosis risk in CHR individuals for clinical trials. Such biomarkers may ultimately prove useful in clinical settings by facilitating identification of those CHR individuals at highest risk for psychosis who, in turn, could be targeted with the most comprehensive and aggressive treatments, and more research is needed to evaluate whether such clinical applications of P300 are justified. Moreover, results support visual P300 inclusion among candidate biomarkers poised to contribute to multimodal multivariate prediction algorithms currently being developed to improve prediction of clinical outcomes in CHR individuals (91), although the potential contributions of visual P300 to these algorithms remains to be fully investigated. Results also support the possible role of visual P300 as a translational neurophysiological target for novel intervention development for the CHR syndrome (92).
