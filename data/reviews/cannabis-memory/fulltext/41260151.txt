Introduction
Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic, progressive autoimmune disorder of the central nervous system (CNS), characterized by CNS inflammation and neurodegeneration, resulting in long-term neurological dysfunction (National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke, n.d.). Cognitive impairment is one of the most common and disabling symptoms of MS, affecting up to 65% of individuals (DiGiuseppe et al., 2018). These impairments—most commonly affecting executive function, processing speed, memory, and attention—are often invisible yet significantly interfere with daily functioning, social participation, and overall quality of life (Halstead et al., 2021; Mitchell et al., 2005). As such, preserving cognitive health is a major priority in the clinical management of MS.
People with MS also present with a broad spectrum of symptoms beyond cognitive dysfunction, including pain (Ehde et al., 2006; Murphy et al., 2017), fatigue (Krupp et al., 2010), spasticity (Stuke et al., 2009), bladder dysfunction (Erden et al., 2022), sleep disturbances (Braley et al., 2012; Braley et al., 2014), anxiety (Boeschoten et al., 2017), and depression (Solaro et al., 2018). These symptoms significantly impact individuals’ personal, professional, and social lives (Bass et al., 2020). As a result, many people with MS rely on multiple medications to manage their symptoms, in addition to disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) aimed at slowing disease progression and reducing relapse frequency (National MS Society, n.d.).
People with MS use more medications than the general population. While approximately 14.5% of U.S. adults take five or more medications—a condition known as polypharmacy (Hales et al., 2019)—this prevalence is notably higher among individuals with MS, ranging from 28% to 56.5% (Chertcoff et al., 2023; Frahm et al., 2019). The most commonly prescribed medications for people with MS include opioid analgesics, antidepressants, antispasmodics, anticonvulsants for neuropathic pain, spasticity, seizure, or migraine, anticholinergics for bladder dysfunction, and benzodiazepines for managing anxiety, insomnia, and spasticity (Hartung et al., 2022). Notably, most of these agents are centrally acting, raising concerns about their potential impact on cognitive function (Stein & Strickland, 1998). In fact, prior research has found that certain CNS-active medications such as antidepressants and benzodiazepines are associated with poorer cognitive performance in MS, particularly in the domains of processing speed and attention (Oken et al., 2006).
Furthermore, polypharmacy has been linked to worse cognitive outcomes in MS—including memory deficits (Thelen et al., 2014), slower processing speed (Atiyeh et al., 2025), and subjective complaints (Atiyeh et al., 2025; Thelen et al., 2014) —with recent evidence suggesting that cumulative exposure to anticholinergic and sedative medications is associated with poorer processing speed and perceived cognitive function (Atiyeh et al., 2025). However, much of the existing literature has focused narrowly on a limited subset of medications, particularly those with anticholinergic or sedative properties (Atiyeh et al., 2025; Cruce et al., 2012), and has rarely examined the effects of other commonly prescribed agents for MS symptoms, such as analgesics or corticosteroids. Moreover, prior studies (Atiyeh et al., 2025; Cruce et al., 2012) have often restricted cognitive outcomes to a single domain, limiting our understanding of how different medications may differentially impact broader aspects of cognition. Understanding the potential impact of medications on broad range of cognitive function is critical, as cognitive impairment occurs across a range of domains in people with MS. To address this gap, the present study analyzed self-reported medication data from an ongoing longitudinal study of cognitive function in MS to assess the association of the use of specific medication classes with both objective and subjective measures across multiple cognitive domains.
Methods
Participants
The present study was a secondary analysis of data from CogDetect-MS study. Full details of the CogDetect-MS protocol are documented elsewhere (Kratz et al., 2024). Eligible adults (≥18) had a confirmed MS diagnosis, English proficiency, and could ambulate independently (with or without assistive devices). Exclusion criteria included recent relapse (within 30 days) or inability to use digital platforms.
Data collection
The CogDetect-MS study was approved by the University of Michigan Medical Institutional Review Board. Participants were recruited and data were collected across three sites: the University of Michigan (Ann Arbor, MI), Wayne State University (Detroit, MI), and the University of Washington (Seattle, WA). Enrollment was facilitated through diverse strategies, including participant databases, electronic medical record reviews, institutional websites, and clinical referrals. Following telephone screening and written informed consent, participants completed baseline assessments, reported current medications, and underwent cognitive testing. Data collection occurred from May 2022 to February 2024.
Measures
Demographic Variables
Structured self-report surveys were administered to collect demographic information, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, education level, marital status, and employment status. Clinical characteristics assessed included MS subtype (checked through medical record review), disease duration, and disability status, as measured by the Patient Determined Disease Steps (PDDS; Learmonth et al., 2013).
Medication Use
At the baseline visit, participants reported all medications they were currently taking, including MS-specific symptom management drugs, medications for comorbid conditions, and disease-modifying therapies (DMTs). Reported medications were categorized using standard classification systems such as Lexicomp and the WHO Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) Index. The list was reviewed and refined in consultation with clinical experts to ensure accuracy and consistency. Based on this process, medications with potential effects on cognitive function, either directly or indirectly through symptom management, were grouped into 11 categories: opioid and non-opioid analgesics (e.g., NSAIDs), cannabinoids, antidepressants (TCAs, SSRIs, SNRIs, others), psychostimulants, antispasmodics, benzodiazepines, sedatives, anticonvulsants (including gabapentinoids), corticosteroids, and DMTs. A comprehensive list of all reported medications, including classification, generic, and brand names, is provided in the Supplementary Table.
Cognitive Function
Cognitive function was assessed using both objective neuropsychological tests and self-reported measures. The NIH Toolbox Cognition Battery (NIHTB-CB), administered via iPad, evaluated multiple domains: executive function (Dimensional Change Card Sort Test [DCCS], Flanker Inhibitory Control and Attention Test [FICA]), working memory (List Sorting Working Memory Test [LSWM]), language (Oral Reading Recognition Test [ORRT], Picture Vocabulary Test [PVT]), processing speed (Pattern Comparison Processing Speed Test [PCPST], Oral Symbol Digit Test [OSD]), and episodic memory (Picture Sequence Memory Test [PSMT]). The NIHTB-CB was selected because it provides a validated, standardized, and efficient assessment of multiple cognitive domains and has been widely used in neurological populations, including MS (Manglani et al., 2022). All scores, except the raw OSD score (range: 0–144), were reported as uncorrected standard scores (range: 40–160; mean = 100, SD = 15). Although uncorrected, models were statistically adjusted for demographic variables (e.g., age, sex, education, PDDS) to allow valid comparisons. Higher scores indicate better cognitive performance in all tests.
To complement the NIHTB-CB, additional validated tests were administered: the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT; range: 0–110) for processing speed, Paced Auditory Serial Addition Test – 3-second version (PASAT; range: 0–60) for working memory, and Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT; range: 0–15) for verbal learning and memory. For these, raw scores were used, with higher scores reflecting better performance. These tests have been widely used in MS populations and are considered sensitive benchmarks for detecting cognitive impairment, thereby strengthening the comprehensiveness and clinical relevance of our cognitive assessment battery (Ntoskou et al., 2018; Rosti et al., 2007; Strober et al., 2019).
Self-reported cognitive function was measured using the PROMIS Cognitive Function Abilities Short Form, a validated 8-item questionnaire assessing perceived cognitive ability over the past week. Responses (1 = not at all to 5 = very much) were converted to standardized T-scores (M = 50, SD = 10), with higher scores indicating better subjective cognition.
Pain Intensity
Pain intensity was assessed using the PROMIS Pain Intensity 3a short form (Cella et al., 2010), which includes three items assessing worst, average, and current pain over the past seven days. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = no pain to 5 = very severe). Raw scores were summed and converted to standardized T-scores (mean = 50, SD = 10), with higher scores indicating greater pain intensity.
Depressive Symptoms
Depressive symptoms were measured using the PROMIS Depression 8b short form (Pilkonis et al., 2011), an 8-item self-report scale assessing the frequency of depressive symptoms over the past seven days. Items are rated on a 5-point scale (1 = never to 5 = always), with raw scores ranging from 8 to 40. These were converted into standardized T-scores (population mean = 50, SD = 10), where higher scores indicate greater depression severity. This measure has demonstrated good validity and reliability in people with MS (Amtmann et al., 2014).
Data Analyses
Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, frequency, proportion) were calculated for all study variables. The total number of medications was also computed, and participants were classified as experiencing polypharmacy if they were taking five or more MS symptomatic medications. Multivariable linear regressions examined the association between each medication category (0 = non-use, 1 = use) and cognitive outcomes, adjusting for age, sex, education, PDDS, and polypharmacy status. Assumptions of linear regression were assessed and met for all models. Analyses were conducted using SPSS 28.0 with α < .05. As this study was exploratory, adjustments for multiple comparisons were not applied (Althouse, 2016).
Sensitivity Analyses
To further examine the robustness of the observed associations between medication use and cognitive outcomes, a series of sensitivity analyses were conducted. Additional regression analyses included symptom severity variables targeted by the medications (e.g., pain for analgesics; depression for antidepressants) as covariates. All other covariates and model specifications were unchanged. This approach allowed us to assess whether the observed relationships between medication use and cognitive function were independent of both the severity of the symptoms the medications were intended to treat and any improvements in those symptoms that may have resulted from taking the medications.
Results
Participants Characteristics
Of the 274 participants enrolled, 260 were included based on available cognitive data. The sample was predominantly female (77.3%) and White (72.7%), with a mean age of 51.0 years (SD = 11.9). Most had relapsing-remitting MS (82.7%), followed by secondary (8.5%) and primary progressive MS (7.3%). Mean disease duration was 15.4 years (SD = 10.0), and the median PDDS score was 2.0, indicating moderate disability. Only six participants showed severe cognitive impairment (≥2 SD below the NIHTB-CB mean), though overall test performance was slightly lower than previously reported in RRMS samples. Detailed characteristics are in Table 1.
Medication Use
On average, participants used 6.5 medications (median = 6; range: 1–22; see Figure 1). Polypharmacy (≥5 medications) was present in 62.7% (n = 163), a rate comparable to that reported in similar MS samples (Frahm et al., 2019). Most participants (69.2%) used DMTs. The most common symptomatic medications were antidepressants (41.9%), followed by non-opioid analgesics and antispasmodics (each 40.8%). Corticosteroids (8.8%) and opioids (6.5%) were least reported. The distribution of medication use is shown in Figure 2. Notably, several combinations of commonly co-prescribed medications were also observed: antidepressants with antispasmodics in 21.5% of participants, DMTs with corticosteroids in 6.2%, and NSAIDs with corticosteroids in 4.2%.
Association of Medication Use with Cognitive Function
Multivariable regression analyses (Table 2) revealed significant associations between specific medications and cognitive performance. Opioid use was linked to poorer executive function (DCCS: β = –0.20, p < .01; FICA: β = −0.12, p = .04). Antidepressant use was positively associated with processing speed (SDMT: β = 0.14, p = .02). In the memory domain, corticosteroid use was linked to enhanced working memory (LSWM: β = 0.14, p = .02), and verbal recall (RAVLT–Immediate: β = 0.14, p = .03; RAVLT–delay: β = 0.13, p = .03). No significant associations were observed for non-opioid analgesics, cannabinoids, psychostimulants, antispasmodics, benzodiazepines, sedatives, anticonvulsants, or DMTs.
Sensitivity Analyses: Adjusted Associations with Cognitive Function
After adjusting for pain intensity, the association between opioid use and poorer executive function (DCCS: β = –0.20, p < .01; FICA: β = −0.12, p = .03) persisted. Likewise, corticosteroid use remained significantly associated with better memory performance (LSWM: β = 0.14, p = .02; RAVLT–Immediate: β = 0.14, p = .03; RAVLT–delay: β = 0.13, p = .03). Additionally, antidepressant use continued to show a positive association with processing speed (SDMT: β = 0.13, p = .03) even after adjusting for depressive symptoms.
Discussion
People with MS often rely on multiple medications to manage their diverse symptoms and MS disease. However, the cognitive implications of these medications have received limited attention. This study is the first to explore the associations between the use of specific medication classes and multiple cognitive domains in people with MS. As expected, the majority of participants in our sample were taking multiple medications. Our findings suggest that the use of certain medications is differentially associated with objective cognitive outcomes across domains such as executive function, processing speed, and memory.
The average number of medications was similar to that reported by Frahm et al. (2019). In our sample, the most commonly reported medications were antidepressants, non-opioid analgesics, and antispasmodics, each used by over 40% of participants, while corticosteroids and opioid analgesics were used by only 8.8 % and 6.5%, respectively. Our findings revealed that opioid analgesic use was significantly associated with poorer executive function, as measured by the DCCS and FICA. In contrast, the use of antidepressants and corticosteroids was associated with better cognitive performance in processing speed and memory.
Notably, earlier studies in MS populations—particularly Medicare beneficiaries—show markedly different medication use patterns compared to the current sample. For example, Hartung et al. (2022) reported that over 50% of Medicare beneficiaries with MS were prescribed opioid analgesics, compared to just 24% using non-opioid options. Several factors may account for this discrepancy. First, the Hartung et al. (2022) sample was older (mean age: 61.5 years) and characterized by a higher burden of chronic comorbidities, which may increase the likelihood of persistent or severe pain requiring opioid treatment (Fiest et al., 2015; Gustavsen et al., 2021). Second, the prescribing data in Hartung et al. (Hartung et al., 2022) were derived from 2014 Medicare claims, reflecting practices that may not align with more recent prescribing trends aimed at reducing opioid use. However, accumulating evidence, including our own, suggests that opioids can negatively impact cognitive function. Chronic opioid use is associated with reduced functional activity and structural changes in the prefrontal cortex—an area critical for executive functioning (Office of the Surgeon General, 2016; Richards et al., 2018). These neurophysiological changes may underlie the poorer performance observed in our study among individuals taking opioid analgesics. Importantly, our sensitivity analyses demonstrated that these associations persisted even after controlling for pain intensity, suggesting that the observed associations with cognitive measures are independent of the severity of underlying pain. This finding indicates that opioids may exert independent, detrimental effects on cognitive functioning beyond their role in symptom control. Although further research is needed to confirm this mechanism, these results highlight the importance of considering cognitive outcomes when selecting pain management strategies in people with MS.
Antidepressant use was significantly associated with better cognitive performance, specifically on measures of processing speed (SDMT). While past research on the cognitive effects of antidepressants—mainly in individuals with major depressive disorder—has yielded mixed results (Keefe et al., 2014; Shilyansky et al., 2016), these inconsistencies may stem from the heterogeneity in pharmacological profiles across different classes of antidepressants, which act on distinct neurochemical pathways involved in cognition. For example, SSRIs such as escitalopram (Wroolie et al., 2006) have been shown to improve cognitive domains such as processing speed and attention. These benefits are thought to stem from increased serotonergic and noradrenergic activity, which enhance synaptic plasticity and neural network efficiency (Lesch & Waider, 2012; Sangkuhl et al., 2009). In contrast, TCAs have not demonstrated similar benefits. In fact, some studies have shown adverse cognitive effects of TCAs. For instance, a trial by Spring et al. (1992) reported that a one-month course of amitriptyline impaired memory performance. This may be explained by TCAs’ strong anticholinergic properties, which interfere with acetylcholine signaling—a neurotransmitter essential for attention and memory (Hasselmo, 2006). In MS populations, anticholinergic burden has similarly been associated with poorer cognitive function (Atiyeh et al., 2025). Our post hoc analyses align with these pharmacologic distinctions: SSRI use was significantly associated with higher SDMT scores (β = 0.13, p = .03), while other antidepressant classes did not show significant associations. In the sensitivity analyses, the associations between antidepressant use and processing speed remained significant even after controlling for depressive symptoms, suggesting that these cognitive benefits are not merely a byproduct of mood improvement but may reflect class-specific mechanisms of action that directly influence cognitive function.
Similarly, participants using corticosteroids demonstrated better cognitive performance. Corticosteroid use was linked to improved multiple memory domains, including working and verbal memory. These findings may be explained by the known anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects of corticosteroids. Beyond inflammation control, emerging experimental evidence suggests that corticosteroids can influence cognitive function directly through modulation of prefrontal region. In a randomized controlled trial, slow-acting hydrocortisone administration improved working memory performance compared with both rapid-acting hydrocortisone and placebo conditions (Henckens et al., 2011). This suggests that a sustained, moderate elevation in glucocorticoid levels rather than acute spikes may optimize neural efficiency and cognitive performance. Although our study also showed a benefit with steroid use, doses, duration, and indication for use were not captured with these data. It is likely that at least some of the participants were using steroids for purposes other than MS, such as chronic peripheral (somatic) pain. Additional research is needed to explore the differential impact of short-term high dose steroids used to treat MS relapses, versus more chronically used lower doses of steroids used for other medical purposes, on cognitive function in MS.
These findings suggest that medication choices in MS should be guided not only by symptom management but also by their potential impact on cognitive function, underscoring the need for an individualized, evidence-informed approach that balances therapeutic benefits with cognitive safety. This is particularly relevant for medications used to treat pain and mood symptoms. Evidence-based non-pharmacologic approaches, such as physiotherapy and structured exercise programs, may serve as effective alternatives to pharmacologic treatment which may reduce reliance on medications and minimize potential cognitive side effects. Although no significant associations were observed between cognitive performance and the use of non-opioid analgesics, cannabinoids, psychostimulants, antispasmodics, benzodiazepines, sedatives, anticonvulsants, or DMTs, these null findings should be interpreted cautiously. Many of these agents target symptoms such as fatigue, spasticity, or sleep disturbance—factors known to influence cognition—and their cognitive effects may have been obscured by variability in dosage, duration, and formulation. For example, benzodiazepines have shown to impair executive function, particularly with short-term use compared to long-term maintenance (Liu et al., 2020), while cannabinoids vary widely in tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and cannabidiol (CBD) content, with higher THC levels associated with greater cognitive impairment (Morgan et al., 2010; Schoedel et al., 2011). In addition, some medications may have been used intermittently (e.g., sedatives for acute insomnia) rather than chronically, increasing heterogeneity. These differences can dilute or cancel out effects in aggregate analyses. Future studies with larger samples and more detailed pharmacologic data are needed to clarify these relationships.
Limitations
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design of this study precludes causal inferences. While we observed associations between specific medication use and cognitive performance, it is not possible to determine whether the medications caused changes in cognition or whether underlying cognitive function or other factors, such as medical comorbidities, influenced medication choices. Longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to clarify the directionality of these relationships. Second, the breadth of medications reported and the modest sample size limited our ability to assess interactions between medications. Given the high prevalence of polypharmacy in MS, interactions among medications are likely to have important implications for cognitive outcomes. Future studies with larger cohorts and sufficient statistical power should evaluate these combined effects, as they may differ substantially from the effects of single medications in isolation. Third, medication data were self-reported, which may be subject to recall bias and reporting errors. In addition, we did not collect information on dosage, treatment duration, or adherence, all of which likely influence cognitive outcomes. Importantly, the study lacked information on the indication for each medication, even though many symptomatic treatments in MS are prescribed for multiple purposes. Future research should incorporate validation of self-reported medication data against clinical or pharmacy records and incorporate both MS-related and comorbidity-related medications with detailed pharmacologic information to provide a more comprehensive understanding of medication-related cognitive outcomes. Fourth, although our sample was relatively large and included racial and gender diversity, participants were generally healthier and more functional than the broader MS population (Frahm et al., 2019). Furthermore, the lack of regional diversity may limit the generalizability of our findings to other geographic or clinical contexts. In addition, our study sample had a high proportion of participants with RRMS, which may constrain the generalizability of findings to people with progressive MS subtypes. As RRMS is typically associated with milder cognitive impairment, our findings may underestimate true effects. Finally, this study focused exclusively on cognitive outcomes. While cognition is a key component of MS-related quality of life, future research should also evaluate medication impacts on fatigue, mood, fall risk, and physical function. Such multidimensional outcomes are essential for developing safe and effective deprescribing guidelines. Overall, these findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Because we did not adjust for multiple comparisons, the possibility of type I error should be acknowledged, and results interpreted with caution. Validation in larger, prospective studies with detailed pharmacologic data is needed.
Conclusion
People with MS use multiple medications to manage diverse MS symptoms and their disease, and our findings indicate that these medications have distinct, domain-specific relationships with cognitive function. These results underscore the importance of considering cognitive effects when making pharmacologic decisions and support an individualized, evidence-based approach to medication management in MS. Future research with detailed pharmacologic data is essential to guide prescribing practices that protect and promote cognitive health alongside symptom control.
