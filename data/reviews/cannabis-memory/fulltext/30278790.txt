Introduction
Adolescence represents a critical juncture in human development, marking the transition from childhood to adulthood (Casey et al., 2008). This period is characterized by adult-like growth across domains, including cognition, which matures into young adulthood to support increasingly complex adult functioning. A combination of neurobiological, genetic, and environmental factors shape brain region and network maturation, leading to improvements in processing speed, inhibitory control, memory, verbal ability, and other domains (Hwang et al., 2010, Luna et al., 2004, Sowell et al., 2001, Thillay et al., 2015, Velanova et al., 2009, Wun et al., 2025). However, adolescence is also a period of heightened vulnerability for psychiatric symptoms (Paus et al., 2008). Many of these symptoms co-occur with impairments in cognition, suggesting complex interactions between cognitive development and youth mental health (Etkin et al., 2013). Despite broad recognition of this relationship, the mechanisms linking cognitive maturation and mental health in youth remain incompletely understood.
Large-scale, publicly available datasets offer a unique opportunity to explore the complex relationships between cognition, mental health symptoms, and adolescent development. Over the past few decades, several “population neuroscience” studies of youth have been established, including the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (n ≈ 11,800) (Volkow et al., 2018), the Imaging, Genetics, and Adolescent Development study (IMAGEN) (n ≈ 2000) (Mascarell Maričić et al., 2020), the Enhancing NeuroImaging Genetics through Meta Analysis (ENIGMA) Consortium (n dependent on image modality and group of interest) (Thompson et al., 2014), the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort (n ≈ 9500) (Satterthwaite et al., 2015), the Orygen Adolescent Development Study (n = 245) (Cotton et al., 2016), and others. Each provides multimodal, cross-sectional, and longitudinal perspectives on neurodevelopment. Among them, the ABCD Study is the largest and most recent, uniquely designed to systematically track childhood development into adolescence. Given its scale, longitudinal design, and the availability of emerging findings—alongside existing retrospectives for other studies (Cotton et al., 2016; Mascarell Maričić et al., 2020; Thompson et al., 2014)—a focused synthesis of ABCD research is timely. Accordingly, this review synthesizes findings from the ABCD Study examining links between cognition and mental health across child and adolescent development.
Cognitive processes underlying mental health
Cognition encompasses memory, attention, executive functioning, processing speed, learning, and language impairments, which are considered a core feature of many psychiatric conditions (Harvey et al., 2004, Kessler et al., 2007, Mulraney et al., 2021). In depression, deficits in working memory and processing speed are thought to be heterogeneous, reflecting variability in symptom profiles and task performance (Baller et al., 2020, Harvey et al., 2004, Landrø et al., 2001, Lee et al., 2012, Rose and Ebmeier, 2006, Semkovska et al., 2019, Snyder, 2013, Tsourtos et al., 2002, Wagner et al., 2015). In schizophrenia (SZ), impairments in working memory and processing speed are robust and evident not only in patients, but also their first-degree relatives, suggesting an endophenotype of cognitive impairment (Badcock et al., 2008, Barch et al., 2002, Conklin et al., 2000, Gooding and Tallent, 2004, Haenschel et al., 2009, Kim et al., 2010, Lee and Park, 2005, Wexler et al., 1998). Anxiety disorders are characterized by disruptions in attentional control and executive functioning, particularly during threat or heightened arousal (Castaneda et al., 2008, Toren et al., 2000). In attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), central deficits in working memory, inhibitory control, and processing speed have been linked to altered prefrontal functioning (Rubia et al., 2021, Yurtbaşı et al., 2015). Notably, working memory and cognitive control impairments in substance use disorders may be pre-morbid, creating vulnerability to substance use disorders (Bechara, 2005, Chanraud et al., 2010, Hanson et al., 2014, Khurana et al., 2017).
Importantly, cognitive impairments may both precede the onset of mental health symptoms, marking risk for future illness, and emerge following symptom onset, suggesting bidirectional relationships between neurocognitive development and mental health symptoms (Choi et al., 2012, Conklin et al., 2000, Cservenka et al., 2011, Cullen et al., 2023, Groenman et al., 2015, Khurana et al., 2013, Mackenzie et al., 2019, Myles-Worsley and Park, 2002, Owen and O’Donovan, 2024, Squeglia and Gray, 2016, Watters et al., 2019). Consistent with this developmental timing, half of all psychiatric illnesses emerge before age 14 (Kessler et al., 2007), and subthreshold mental health problems in childhood portend adult mental illness (Mulraney et al., 2021). The tight coupling of cognition and mental health across development has therefore motivated investigations of their dynamic interplay during childhood and adolescence. At the time of the ABCD Study’s initiation, cognition–psychopathology associations were well documented, but their developmental timing, directionality, and underlying neural and genetic mechanisms remained unclear.
The ABCD Study
The ABCD Study’s large, demographically diverse, longitudinal cohort enables in-depth examination of neurocognitive development and mental health. The study follows over 11,000 children recruited at ages 9–10 years old across 21 U.S. sites for up to 10 years and integrates neuroimaging, neurocognitive testing, genetic data, and youth- and parent-reported measures of mental health and substance use (Barch et al., 2018, Karcher and Barch, 2021, Luciana et al., 2018). Behavioral and self-report assessments are collected annually, with neuroimaging acquired biennially (Casey et al., 2018). This scale provides statistical power to detect subtle effects and developmental heterogeneity while improving reproducibility in developmental neuroscience (Button et al., 2013, Garavan et al., 2018, Saragosa-Harris et al., 2022). Data-driven approaches that identify latent variables, predictive models, and longitudinal models are particularly well-suited for the ABCD Study, given its large size. Furthermore, the ABCD Study’s breadth allows for a transdiagnostic approach to youth mental health, as multiple symptom domains and a comprehensive cognitive battery are concurrently assessed within the same large cohort. In contrast to prior studies that often focused on a single diagnosis, symptom domain, or isolated cognitive task, ABCD provides an opportunity to examine whether cognitive vulnerabilities are disorder-specific, reflect broader transdiagnostic dimensions, or vary across developmental stages. This integrative structure is particularly valuable given high comorbidity and symptom overlap in youth psychopathology (Merikangas et al., 2010).
ABCD Study cognitive measures
The ABCD Study assesses cognition using the NIH Toolbox Cognition Battery (NIHTB-CB), which measures learning, memory, executive function, processing speed, and language and reading (Luciana et al., 2018). Additional tasks assess fluid reasoning, visuospatial abilities, and the influence of emotion on cognition (Smolker et al., 2022). Task-based functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) paradigms, including the Stop Signal Task (SST) and Emotional N-back Task (EN-back), probe inhibitory control and working memory, respectively (Casey et al., 2018). Collectively, these measures provide a multidimensional assessment of cognitive functioning from late childhood through adolescence. This article
To our knowledge, no prior review has synthesized findings from the ABCD Study examining cognitive functioning in relation to mental health. The present systematic review integrates this rapidly growing literature, spanning both baseline and longitudinal analyses. At the time of writing, published studies drew on baseline through 3-year follow-up data, as well as select 4-year follow-up data, from the National Institute of Mental Health Data Archive (NDA) 5.1 release or earlier. Relevant papers were identified through systematic searches of PubMed, Google Scholar, PsycInfo, ProQuest, Scopus, and the ABCD Study research publications database (https://abcdstudy.org/research-publications/). Search terms combined “ABCD Study” with keywords “cognition,” “mental health,” “psychopathology,” “neuroimaging,” and “genetics.” Reference lists were additionally screened to identify eligible studies. Only peer-reviewed, English-language articles published through November 2025 were included. Examining cognition and mental health was the anchoring point of every selected article. Papers were eligible if they examined: (1) associations between cognition and mental health symptoms; (2) relationships among cognition, mental health, and neuroimaging measures; or (3) relationships among cognition, mental health, and genetic factors, at baseline and/or longitudinal timepoints (Fig. 1). CBCL externalizing, internalizing, and general problems
Behavioral
Baseline
Most studies cross-sectionally examining the relationship between cognition and mental health at baseline utilized cognitive tasks from the NIHTB-CB and mental health measures from the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL). Across studies, more severe externalizing symptoms were associated with lower cognitive ability, a finding that was consistently replicated, despite the variety of analytic approaches. Though this finding was consistent, effect sizes were often small or very small (Table 4). Studies identifying latent cognitive factors reflecting general cognition, memory, and executive functioning demonstrated an inverse relationship between these factors and externalizing symptoms, regardless of the method for deriving latent factors (Moore and Conway, 2023; Thompson et al., 2019). Observable (i.e., non-latent) crystallized (e.g., language, vocabulary, reading) and fluid (e.g., executive function, cognitive flexibility, episodic memory) cognitive variables also had negative relationships with externalizing symptoms (Steinberger and Barch, 2021b). When taking the approach of deriving latent profiles of externalizing symptoms from the CBCL, studies confirmed the negative relationship with cognitive performance, with intriguing sex differences, as boys performed worse than girls at comparable symptom severity (Blok et al., 2023). Similarly, worse reading ability, episodic memory, and verbal ability, but faster processing speed, predicted membership in a latent class with higher externalizing symptoms (Retzler et al., 2023). Only Brislin et al. (2022) observed a slightly different pattern—there was no association between a latent externalizing and latent cognitive (g-factor) factor, but a negative relationship between observable externalizing symptoms and observable cognitive scores. Independent of the analytic approach, these studies consistently found an inverse relationship between cognitive performance and externalizing symptoms.
In contrast, findings for internalizing symptoms were mixed, with several studies showing a negative relationship with executive functioning, but a positive relationship with verbal ability. As with studies of externalizing symptoms, these findings were consistent irrespective of methodology, and most effect sizes were very small (Table 4). Both a latent factor and observable T-scores of internalizing symptoms were negatively associated with latent or observable scores of fluid cognition (e.g., executive functioning and working memory) and positively associated with latent or observable scores of crystallized cognition (e.g., vocabulary, language) (Steinberger and Barch, 2021b; W. K. Thompson et al., 2019). Similarly, worse episodic and working memory, but better verbal ability, predicted membership in a latent class defined by greater internalizing symptoms (Retzler et al., 2023). While the reasons for enhanced verbal ability are unclear, authors posited that better verbal skills may enhance caregivers’ ability to detect internalizing symptoms, which are often less overt and more difficult to observe (Steinberger and Barch, 2021b). Separately, Brislin et al. (2022) found a positive association between a latent internalizing factor and an overall g-factor derived from a bifactor model of all cognitive scores, somewhat conflicting with the differential relationship between internalizing symptoms and cognitive domains found in other studies. Finally, Vedechkina and colleagues (2024) raised the intriguing possibility that externalizing symptoms may underlie the negative association between executive functioning and internalizing symptoms. Negative associations between a latent internalizing factor and inhibitory control (NIHTB-CB Flanker) and set shifting (NIHTB-CB Dimensional Change) performance were no longer significant after controlling for externalizing symptoms (Vedechkina et al., 2024). Notably, analyses of other non-ABCD datasets have found both positive (Karpinski et al., 2018, Kermarrec et al., 2020, Penney et al., 2015) and negative relationships (Abramovitch et al., 2021, Blanken et al., 2016, Snyder, 2013, Williams et al., 2023) between verbal performance and internalizing symptoms in children, highlighting an area in which unique aspects of the ABCD Study, particularly its large sample size and longitudinal design, may help clarify conflicting findings in the broader literature.
Several studies analyzed whether cognitive functioning was linked to a single factor underlying all psychiatric conditions (a common ‘p-factor’). This general p-factor accounted for a small amount of variance in fluid and crystallized intelligence in the baseline ABCD data (Michelini et al., 2019). Bifactor and higher-order modeling of the CBCL demonstrated that higher p-factor scores were associated with lower latent executive functioning scores (Cardenas-Iniguez et al., 2022, Lees et al., 2021; Moore et al., 2020; Romer and Pizzagalli, 2021). Similarly, Retzler and colleagues (2023) found that worse working memory (NIHTB-CB List Sorting Working Memory), reading ability (NIHTB-CB Oral Reading Recognition) and episodic memory (NIHTB-CB Picture Sequence) predicted membership in a latent class reflecting generalized difficulties (akin to a p-factor). In contrast, Wiker et al. (2023) observed no significant relationship between reaction time variability on the SST and a general p-factor. Overall, effect sizes were very small (Table 4), and different approaches to identifying latent factors may further complicate interpretations of these findings (Clark et al., 2021; Moore et al., 2020). In general, results from the ABCD Study confirmed previous large cohort studies that have linked impaired executive functioning in development to a common factor underlying psychiatric conditions (Bloemen et al., 2018, Martel et al., 2017, Patalay et al., 2015, Shields et al., 2019, Waldman et al., 2016).
Finally, other work highlighted the complex, often non-linear, associations between cognitive functioning and psychiatric symptomology. While NIHTB-CB cognitive performance was negatively associated with mental health symptoms among children without early life adversity, it was not related to symptom severity among those exposed to early life adversity (Vedechkina and Holmes, 2024). This pattern suggests that, for those with early life adversity, cognitive performance may explain little additional variance in psychiatric symptom development (Vedechkina and Holmes, 2024). Relatedly, Pines and colleagues (2024) identified inverted-U-shaped relationships between CBCL symptom scores and cognition. For those with lower symptoms, greater CBCL total scores predicted higher scores on a cognitive principal component, while for those with higher symptom severity, greater CBCL scores predicted lower cognitive scores (Pines et al., 2024). This non-linear pattern generalized to both internalizing and externalizing domains, though it was stronger for internalizing symptoms, suggesting a possible explanation for conflicting findings on the relationship between cognition and internalizing symptoms (Pines et al., 2024). Despite the small effect sizes, these non-linear patterns suggest that collapsing across severity levels may obscure meaningful effects; uniquely, the large sample size of the ABCD Study enables well-powered stratified analyses.
Longitudinal
Only a few studies examined whether cognitive scores at baseline were associated with future externalizing, internalizing, or general psychiatric symptoms. All effect sizes were very small in magnitude (Table 4), and only one study extended analyses to the 3-year follow-up timepoint. Paskewitz et al. (2024) found that latent cognitive profiles characterized by below-average baseline cognitive performance across tasks were related to higher externalizing symptoms at 2- and 3-year follow-ups. Inverted-U-shaped relationships were observed between baseline fluid intelligence (NIHTB-CB) and two-year changes in internalizing and externalizing symptoms. Specifically, children with moderate baseline fluid intelligence scores exhibited the greatest increases in symptoms over two years, compared to peers with lower or higher scores (Li et al., 2025). Moderate fluid intelligence also predicted a higher likelihood of developing elevated externalizing symptoms among those with low baseline symptoms, as well as persistence of internalizing symptoms among those with high baseline internalizing symptoms (Li et al., 2025). Replication in the IMAGEN dataset supported this pattern, revealing an inverted-U relationship between IQ (Wechler Intelligence Scale for Children) and externalizing symptom growth (Li et al., 2025). Finally, at the 2-year follow-up timepoint, Romer and Pizzagalli (2021) found bidirectional relationships between a general psychopathology p-factor and a combined executive functioning factor derived from subtests of the NIHTB-CB. Lower scores on the executive functioning factor at baseline predicted increases in p-factor scores at the 2-year follow-up—in turn, higher p-factor scores at baseline predicted subsequent weaker executive functioning (Romer and Pizzagalli, 2021). This work, together with findings from Pines and colleagues, suggests the need to consider executive functioning as both a potential risk factor for, and a consequence of, broad psychopathology.
Neuroimaging
Baseline
Numerous studies linked brain structure or function with cognition and psychopathology using baseline data from the ABCD Study, generally with very small effect sizes (Table 4). Structural features of the cortex, including surface area and volume were positively associated with cognitive performance and negatively associated with general psychopathology, especially in prefrontal and temporal regions (Patel et al., 2022, Romer et al., 2023). Mediation analyses demonstrated that cognition partially mediated (20–40% of the effect) the link between cortical surface area and mental health symptoms, and mental health symptoms modestly mediated (6–13% of the effect) the association between surface area and cognition (Patel et al., 2022). Similarly, Mewton et al. (2022) suggested that cognition may partially mediate the relationship between cortical morphology and symptom severity, as adjusting for cognitive performance substantially attenuated the negative relationship between global cortical surface area/volume and general mental health symptoms. While the aforementioned studies did not find relationships between cortical thickness, cognition and psychopathology, Yu et al. (2023) found that increased cortical thickness in the pars opercularis among children with internalizing and externalizing disorders was associated with weaker NIHTB-CB reading and working memory. Collectively, the relationships between altered cortical structure, worse cognition and psychopathology were of very small effect size (Table 4).
Studies incorporating fMRI used a variety of approaches to examine relationships between cognition, mental health, and brain functioning. DeRosa et al. (2024) applied a machine-learning framework and found that subtypes of children with positive resting-state default mode and cingulo-opercular within-network connectivity had higher cognitive scores and fewer mental health problems, whereas subtypes marked by negative default mode and cingulo-opercular within-network connectivity exhibited weaker cognition and more symptoms. For task-based analyses, those with greater internalizing, externalizing, or thought disorder symptoms had lower BOLD activation in the middle frontal gyrus during the EN-back task (Lees et al., 2021). For the SST, greater irritability was associated with reduced right rolandic operculum activation during successful inhibition, as well as decreased connectivity across cortico-amygdala and striatal-frontal circuits; these associations persisted after controlling for additional CBCL symptom domains, suggesting that irritability specifically accounted for the observed neural differences (Parker et al., 2025).
A few studies leveraged predictive approaches to identify neuroimaging features that predicted cognition and mental health symptoms. Functional connectivity during the EN-back and resting-state connectivity of default mode and frontoparietal control networks significantly predicted mental health symptoms and cognition at baseline (Chen et al., 2022). Better white matter integrity, measured using fractional anisotropy from diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), in the inferior longitudinal fasciculus and parietal superior corticostriate tracts, along with weaker reading (NIHTB-CB Oral Reading Recognition) and episodic memory (NIHTB-CB Picture Sequence) performance, were the strongest predictors of membership in a latent class characterized by externalizing symptoms (Retzler et al., 2023). In another study, Wang et al. (2025) found that neuroimaging features and mental health symptoms (composite of the CBCL, Behavioral Inhibition System/Behavioral Activation System Scale, and UPPS-P Impulsive Behavior Scale) explained 27% of the variance in a latent general cognition factor derived from the NIHTB-CB, with 6.48% shared variance between the two domains.
Longitudinal
Brain development appeared to mediate cognitive predictors of future internalizing and externalizing symptoms, with very small effect sizes (Table 4). For those with high externalizing symptoms at baseline, changes in left occipital cortex volumetric growth mediated the effects of fluid intelligence on symptom trajectories at the 2-year follow-up (Li et al., 2025). Similarly, for those with high internalizing symptoms at baseline, volumetric growth in frontal and occipital cortices mediated the effects of fluid intelligence on symptom trajectories two years later (Li et al., 2025). Adding fluid intelligence scores to neuroimaging predictive models improved the prediction of late-onset (2–3-year follow-up) externalizing symptoms in those with low externalizing symptoms at baseline and the prediction of internalizing symptom remission for those with high internalizing symptoms at baseline (Li et al., 2025). Better attention shifting (NIHTB-CB Dimensional Change) was associated with a trajectory of decreasing internalizing symptoms between 10–12-years old, specifically in children with relatively higher frontoparietal-amygdala functional connectivity at baseline (Gunther et al., 2023). Finally, Xie and colleagues (2023) used the ABCD dataset to validate a latent “neuropsychopathological factor” derived from IMAGEN data that centered on inhibitory control (SST) and reward anticipation-provoked frontoparietal and precuneus functional connectivity. In both datasets, higher scores on this general neural factor at baseline predicted greater externalizing and internalizing symptoms one year later, highlighting a common neural mechanism that may underlie mental health symptoms (Xie et al., 2023). Given how few studies focused on the neural mechanisms of longitudinal cognitive–symptom relationships, this may be a domain for future studies.
Genetics
Baseline
Very limited work has utilized genetics data to understand cognitive–symptom relationships. Wang et al. (2025) conducted commonality analyses to find that 21% of the covariance between cognition and mental health was shared with cognitive polygenic scores (PGSs), demonstrating that genetic variations underlie some of the relationship between cognition and mental health.
Longitudinal
Li and colleagues (2025) conducted a novel genome-wide association study (GWAS) using fluid intelligence scores and inferior frontal and occipital cortical volume changes in high-symptom participants as phenotypes. Analyses revealed enrichment for genes related to synaptic function and pruning, particularly ADCY1, suggesting that genetic variation in synaptic biology may be linked to the neural and cognitive processes that shape symptom trajectories (Li et al., 2025).
Anxiety and mood disorders
Behavioral
Baseline
The few baseline studies on anxiety and depression parsed heterogeneity in cognition–symptom relationships, an illuminating approach given the lack of relationship between depression and cognition found in another baseline analysis (Steinberger and Barch, 2021a). Ang and colleagues (2020) used the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia (KSADS−5) and Family History Assessment Module Screener (FHAM-S) to classify children as currently depressed, remitted, low-risk and healthy (no parental depression), or high-risk and healthy (parental depression). Children with current or remitted depression showed impaired language and reasoning, possibly reflecting both state- and trait-related features of childhood depression (Ang et al., 2020). The lack of impairments in high-risk, but unaffected, youth suggested that such deficits may not precede symptom onset (Ang et al., 2020), though a prior meta-analysis reported cognitive impairments in individuals at risk for depression, albeit across a much broader age range (Mackenzie et al., 2019).
For anxiety, Fan and colleagues (2023) applied hierarchical clustering to CBCL data and identified two subtypes, both characterized by high levels of anxiety, but with either low or high impulsivity. The high anxiety/high impulsivity group had lower vocabulary, reading, crystallized intelligence, and overall NIHTB-CB scores, compared to both the high anxiety/low impulsivity and healthy groups (Fan et al., 2023). A separate study identified an attentional subtype, for which cognitive impairments among anxious or depressed children were attributed to attentional dysregulation (Roberts et al., 2023). Compared to peers without symptoms, children with anxiety or depression who did not have attentional dysregulation had comparable fluid and higher crystallized cognition scores (Roberts et al., 2023).
Longitudinal
Like baseline studies, few studies examined longitudinal relationships between cognition and anxiety or depression, mostly with very small effect sizes (Table 4). Higher baseline crystallized cognition predicted greater depressive symptoms (CBCL) and greater child-reported depressive diagnoses (KSADS−5) two years later, although not greater parent-reported diagnoses (Steinberger and Barch, 2021a). No significant relationships were found between baseline fluid intelligence scores and depression two years later (Steinberger and Barch, 2021a). In contrast, another study found that lower baseline inhibitory control scores (NIHTB-CB Flanker) were associated with increased latent anxiety, depression, and withdrawal factor scores from the CBCL two years later (Vedechkina et al., 2024). Better baseline cognitive shifting (NIHTB-CB Dimensional Change) was associated with higher anxiety two years later. Notably, effects were attenuated when externalizing symptoms were controlled for, highlighting likely contributions from these symptoms (Vedechkina et al., 2024).
These findings echo mixed results from other large cohort analyses on cognition and anxiety/depression. While one study found significant relationships between adolescent depression and later cognitive performance (China Family Panel Study; Wang et al., 2023), others (Dunedin and Environmental Risk [E-Risk] Longitudinal Twin Studies) found that depression did not confer long-lasting cognitive deficits unless comorbid with other psychiatric conditions, and that low childhood cognition did not predict later depression or anxiety (Cannon et al., 2002, Schaefer et al., 2017). In the Canadian National Longitudinal Study of Children and Youth (NLSCY), stronger childhood cognitive performance protected against internalizing symptoms in early adolescence, but had null, or even positive, associations by mid-adolescence (Weeks et al., 2014). Examination of forthcoming longitudinal ABCD data will be critical for clarifying directionality, developmental timing, and possible subgroups (as done with the baseline data) to elucidate longitudinal relationships between cognition and anxiety and mood disorders.
Neuroimaging
Baseline
Some baseline studies described earlier also examined associated neural circuits. Ang and colleagues (2020) applied canonical correlation analysis and identified a first canonical variate linking a language and reasoning factor to brain volumes in language processing regions, including the middle temporal gyrus, superior frontal gyrus, and superior parietal cortex. Children with current depression had smaller volumes in these regions, along with lower language/reasoning scores, compared to low-risk, high-risk, and remitted groups, who all had similar regional volumes (Ang et al., 2020). In Fan and colleagues (2023), preadolescents with high anxiety/high impulsivity and lower cognitive performance exhibited greater cortical thickness in prefrontal and temporal regions relative to peers with high anxiety/low impulsivity or controls. These studies suggest that differences in cortical structure may distinguish subgroups of depressed or anxious youth who have cognitive impairment, highlighting the importance of characterizing heterogeneity rather than searching for a uniform neural signature.
Longitudinal
No studies were found.
Genetics
Baseline
Comparing ABCD and UK Biobank data, Gui and colleagues (2022) reported sex-specific associations between MDD polygenic risk scores (PRSs) and cognition. In boys, higher MDD PRSs were associated with lower reading ability, crystallized intelligence, and total NIHTB-CB performance, while no significant associations were observed in girls. Bipolar disorder (BD) PRSs were not significantly related to cognition (Gui et al., 2022). Complementing these findings, Pat et al. (2022a) found that a NIHTB-CB-derived cognitive g-factor mediated associations between major depressive disorder (MDD) PGSs and a general p-factor, as well as between externalizing, somatoform, neurodevelopmental, and internalizing CBCL symptoms. In contrast, Pedersen et al. (2023), using Bayesian multilevel models, found no significant associations between BD or MDD PRSs and either EN-back performance or a general cognitive ability index (first NIHTB-CB principal component).
Longitudinal
No studies were found.
ADHD and disruptive behavior disorders
Behavioral
Baseline
Using a variety of modeling approaches, several papers measured negative associations between ADHD or disruptive symptoms and cognitive domains, often of very small or small effect sizes (Table 4). Inverse associations were observed between a cognitive factor from the NIHTB-CB and ADHD or conduct-related latent factors from the CBCL (Cardenas-Iniguez et al., 2022; Moore et al., 2020), with Moore and colleagues notably reporting a medium effect size. Increasingly stringent ADHD diagnostic criteria—excluding comorbidities and requiring multi-informant agreement of symptoms—were associated with a graded pattern of cognitive impairment such that mean scores for general cognitive ability, executive function, working memory, and fluid intelligence declined as latent ADHD factor scores increased (Cordova et al., 2022). For those with ADHD, a greater ability to use goal-relevant information when making decisions (i.e., more efficient evidence accumulation), as measured with drift diffusion models applied to the EN-back task, was associated with fewer parent- or teacher-reported attentional problems on the CBCL or Brief Problem Monitor (BPM-Y), respectively (Weigard et al., 2023)
Studies involving other disruptive symptoms and diagnoses also found associations with cognition, though findings were less consistent than for ADHD. Weaker executive function, learning, and memory predicted greater ADHD, conduct disorder (CD), and ODD symptoms, while lower overall cognitive ability was linked to more parent-reported ADHD and CD diagnoses (Murtha et al., 2025). Co-varying for callous-unemotional (CU) traits did not change these findings, but in moderation models, lower general cognitive ability was more strongly related to CD in children with the highest CU traits (Murtha et al., 2025). These findings somewhat conflicted with prior studies showing that better cognition in youth with CU traits was associated with greater externalizing or delinquent behaviors (Baskin-Sommers et al., 2015, de Graaf et al., 2023, Dotterer et al., 2021, Graziano et al., 2022, Muñoz et al., 2008). In contrast, Lee and colleagues (2022) found no differences in response inhibition (SST) across four latent classes characterized by distinct profiles of ADHD symptoms and irritability (KSADS−5). Finally, in children born preterm, greater ADHD symptoms and lower scores on vocabulary, reading ability, fluid reasoning, crystallized intelligence, and overall cognitive composites were associated with lower gestational age and birth weight, demonstrating possible interactions with perinatal factors (Ji et al., 2024).
Longitudinal
Earlier cognitive performance informed future ADHD symptoms during early adolescence. When reported, effect sizes for longitudinal associations between cognition and ADHD symptoms were small or very small (Table 4). Weaker executive functioning (EN-back), verbal ability, and inhibitory control (NIHTB-CB Flanker and SST) at baseline were associated with greater ADHD symptoms two years later (Pang et al., 2025, Weigard et al., 2023). Two studies examined bidirectional longitudinal effects (i.e., cognition → future ADHD symptoms, ADHD symptoms → future cognition). McKay et al. (2024) found that higher baseline ADHD symptoms were significantly associated with worse inhibitory control (NIHTB-CB Flanker) at the 2-year follow-up; however, baseline inhibitory control was not associated with ADHD symptoms or irritability at the 2-year follow-up. In contrast, Pang and colleagues (2025) used a bivariate change model to show that smaller improvements in inhibitory control (NIHTB-CB Flanker) were associated with greater increases in ADHD symptoms over two years, while the reverse relationship (ADHD → NIHTB-CB Flanker) was not significant. Finally, preadolescents with low effortful control and executive functioning, as measured by the Early Adolescent Temperament Questionnaire and the Barkley Deficits in Executive Functioning Scale—Children and Adolescents (BDEFS-CA), at the 2-year follow-up showed a positive association between familial risk for ADHD and later ADHD symptoms at the 3-year follow-up; those with high effortful control and executive functioning showed no such association (Peisch et al., 2023).
Longitudinal studies also examined whether baseline cognition was associated with later disruptive disorders and symptoms. Worse executive functioning, learning, and memory at baseline predicted greater overt aggression at the 2-year follow-up, though better general cognition, learning, and memory at baseline unexpectedly predicted higher relational aggression (Murtha et al., 2025). Using a feedforward neural network in their predictive model, Chan and colleagues (2023) found that lower baseline scores on NIHTB-CB crystallized cognition, cognitive flexibility (Dimensional Change), and attention (Flanker) were among several biopsychosocial predictors that significantly predicted a CD diagnosis at the 2-year follow-up. These findings align with prior work on the difficulty that youth with CD have adapting to new rules and acquiring verbal knowledge (Matthys et al., 2012, Moffitt, 1993).
Neuroimaging
Baseline
Available baseline neuroimaging studies on ADHD and cognition mostly examined task-based fMRI paradigms and DWI, finding disruptions in networks associated with cognitive control and task-based activity. Using the SST, Lee and colleagues (2022) applied exploratory factor analysis to identify a latent co-activation cortical network that co-varied with response inhibition and had reduced co-activation in children with high irritability/low ADHD symptoms, compared to typically developing peers and children with high irritability/high ADHD symptoms. A similarly identified error response network did not show group differences in co-activation (Lee et al., 2022). For the working memory EN-back task, BOLD activation differentiated children with and without ADHD in the left frontal gyri, right supra-marginal gyrus, anterior cingulate cortex, parietal cortices, and hippocampus (Pornpattananangkul et al., 2019). Another study found that EN-back neural activation predicted CBCL ADHD symptomatology more accurately than SST neural activation, and higher ADHD symptoms were associated with reduced activation in task-positive regions (i.e., dorsolateral PFC, posterior parietal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex) and increased activation in task-negative/default-mode regions (i.e., ventromedial PFC, posterior cingulate cortex, lateral temporal cortex, precentral and postcentral gyri) (Owens et al., 2021). Likewise, reduced temporal complexity and less efficient neural processing (indexed by the Hurst exponent (H)) during the EN-back task were associated with higher ADHD-specific scores, and weaker working memory performance (Stier et al., 2023). This corresponded to reduced activation in frontoparietal control regions, but increased activation in sensorimotor regions, possibly reflecting inefficient allocation of cognitive resources (Stier et al., 2023). Finally, the association between sociodemographic and psychological factors, including attention symptoms, and a general cognitive factor, was mediated by brain-based predictive models (Pat et al., 2022b).
With DWI data, Cardenas-Iniguez and colleagues (2022) used structural equation modeling to reveal that greater white matter integrity (indexed by higher fractional anisotropy or lower mean diffusivity) was linked to better executive functioning, which in turn was associated with fewer ADHD symptoms and conduct problems. Fractional anisotropy effects localized to the corpus callosum and superior longitudinal and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculi, whereas mean diffusivity effects occurred broadly across most examined white matter tracts, suggesting that factors affecting general microstructure may impact executive functioning and contribute to ADHD or conduct problems (Cardenas-Iniguez et al., 2022). Wiker et al. (2023) found that greater variability in reaction time on the SST was associated with more attention problems and higher radial diffusivity in the corticospinal tracts, suggesting that possible immature white matter organization underlies inefficient response control. Altered connectivity and neural dynamics across these systems—ranging from reduced microstructural integrity to less efficient temporal complexity and network co-activation—appeared to underlie the attentional and inhibitory control difficulties characteristic of ADHD. The lack of neuroimaging studies on disruptive disorders points to an area for future work.
Longitudinal
Longitudinal analyses tested whether similar neural mechanisms prospectively predict the course of ADHD or disruptive behavior disorders. Mediation analyses found that neural inefficiency, indexed by lower H in frontoparietal regions during the EN-back task at baseline, partially explained the relationship between baseline ADHD symptoms and later cognitive performance, highlighting a potential mechanism through which altered large-scale neural dynamics forecast cognitive and behavioral trajectories (Stier et al., 2023). For CD, lower baseline crystallized cognition, cognitive flexibility, resting-state subcortical efficiency, and frontoparietal connectedness, as well as greater frontoparietal network efficiency, were among the key predictors of illness at the 2-year follow-up in a feed-forward neural network (Chan et al., 2023). This is consistent with previous studies showing that inhibition, flexibility, and rule use rely on efficient integration between subcortical nodes and frontoparietal control systems (Cohn et al., 2015, Finger et al., 2011, Zhou et al., 2015). Similarly, de Lacy and Ramshaw (2023) employed an AI-based predictive modeling framework combining machine learning with automated feature selection to predict prevailing or new-onset ADHD, ODD, and CD at the 2-year follow-up. For ADHD and ODD, the best-performing models primarily relied on non-neural features, though neural-only models still achieved strong predictive accuracy, with left ventrolateral prefrontal activation during inhibitory control (SST stop vs. go) as one of the predictors of new-onset ADHD at two years (de Lacy and Ramshaw, 2023). For CD, optimal models incorporated neural predictors to predict persistent CD at 2-year follow-up (de Lacy and Ramshaw, 2023). Taken together, these studies indicate that deficits in executive functioning—including those involving frontoparietal and subcortical network maturation—contribute to the emergence and persistence of ADHD, ODD, and CD, with multimodal models showing strong potential for forecasting later illness.
Genetics
Baseline
Baseline analyses indicated that genetic liability for ADHD is associated with both cognitive performance and behavioral symptoms. Lower general cognitive ability (NIHTB-CB) partially mediated the association between higher ADHD PGSs and greater externalizing and neurodevelopmental symptoms (Pat et al., 2022a). Sha and colleagues (2025) examined the burden of copy number variants (CNVs) by creating a CNV risk score for global and dimensional psychopathology (CBCL) and cognition (NIHTB-CB). Deletion-based CNV risk scores were associated with greater attentional problems and worse overall cognition across several domains (Sha et al., 2025).
Longitudinal
Across studies, higher baseline ADHD PRSs were associated with weaker working memory (EN-back), lower executive functioning (NIHTB-CB), greater reaction time variability (SST), and more ADHD symptoms during follow-up timepoints, with very small effect sizes (Table 4). However, evidence that cognition mediates a genetic–behavioral association was mixed (Lahey et al., 2022, Moses et al., 2022, Pang et al., 2025). ADHD PRSs and inhibitory control PRSs derived from a GWAS of SST performance were correlated with baseline CBCL ADHD symptoms, as well as their rate of change at the 2-year and 4-year follow-ups (Pang et al., 2025). Working memory and reaction time variability partially explained the relationship between baseline ADHD PRSs and ADHD traits (CBCL and Early Adolescent Questionnaire-Revised) at the 2-year follow-up, suggesting these domains may represent cognitive endophenotypes through which genetic risk manifests as later symptom expression (Moses et al., 2022). In contrast, Lahey and colleagues (2022) reported that, while a latent executive function factor (NIHTB-CB Pattern Comparison, Dimensional Change, and Flanker tasks) was associated with higher ADHD PRSs and latent ADHD and CD symptom factors at baseline and 1-year follow-up, it did not significantly mediate the relationship between ADHD PRSs and later ADHD or CD problems. Together, these studies suggest that impaired working memory, reaction time variability, and executive functioning may serve as intermediate phenotypes between polygenic risk for ADHD and behavioral expression. However, evidence for mediation remains limited, highlighting the need for longitudinal, integrative models that more precisely capture genetic–cognitive–behavioral pathways in ADHD.
Obsessive-compulsive symptoms
Behavioral
Baseline
Higher OCS scores (CBCL) at baseline were modestly associated with weaker performance on NIHTB-CB cognitive control, cognitive flexibility, working memory, and episodic memory tasks, but better picture vocabulary performance (Pagliaccio et al., 2021). However, after adjusting for ADHD symptom scores (CBCL), or lifetime ADHD diagnoses (KSADS−5) and ADHD medication use, higher OCS scores were associated with overall better cognitive scores, largely driven by stronger working memory, picture vocabulary, and oral reading performance (Pagliaccio et al., 2021). Moreover, children with a lifetime diagnosis of OCD showed better cognitive performance than those with ADHD or comorbid ADHD and OCD, further demonstrating the impact of comorbid ADHD on cognitive impairments in OCD (Pagliaccio et al., 2021).
Longitudinal
Few ABCD studies examined longitudinal cognition in youth with OCS. Wu and colleagues (2022) applied consensus hierarchical clustering and identified a low anxiety/high compulsion group with significantly lower scores on all NIHTB-CB tasks at baseline compared to the high anxiety/low compulsion and typical groups. A high anxiety/low compulsion group had higher scores on picture vocabulary, reading, and crystallized intelligence subcomponents, compared to the typical group (Wu et al., 2022). These relationships persisted at the 2- and 4-year follow-ups (Wu et al., 2022). The low anxiety/high compulsion group additionally had higher externalizing symptoms, while the high anxiety/low compulsion group had higher internalizing symptoms (Wu et al., 2022), echoing findings between cognition and internalizing/externalizing symptoms reviewed earlier. In contrast, Pagliaccio and colleagues (2021) found that baseline cognitive performance did not significantly predict changes in OCS scores at the 1-year follow-up. Overall, findings from the ABCD Study mirror the inconsistent results of previous studies examining cognitive deficits in children with OCS, while also suggesting that cognitive difficulties may partly reflect comorbid symptoms, rather than OCD-specific effects.
Neuroimaging
Baseline
No studies were found.
Longitudinal
Longitudinal studies examining brain–cognition–symptom relationships in youth with OCS remain limited, with very small effect sizes (Table 4). In the low anxiety/high compulsion group identified by Wu and colleagues (2022), baseline cortical volume in the inferior parietal lobe and the precuneus was negatively associated with compulsive symptoms and crystallized intelligence scores at the 2-year follow-up. In contrast, Pagliaccio and colleagues (2021) found that NIHTB-CB measures and superior corticostriatal tract fractional anisotropy, which were associated with baseline OCS scores, did not significantly predict change in OCS scores at the 1-year follow-up. Overall, literature directly examining the relationship among neurobiology, cognition, and OCS symptoms remains sparse, pointing to an area for further study.
Genetics
Baseline
Using Bayesian multilevel linear models, Pedersen et al. (2023) did not find significant associations between OCD PRSs and any cognitive measures (EN-back performance and a first principal component of the NIHTB-CB).

Suicidality
Behavioral
Baseline
Baseline studies examining suicidality indicated small, but reliable, cognitive effects, with modest classification performance and domain-specific effects rather than broad cognitive impairment. Children across all levels of suicidality (caregiver- and child-reported KSADS−5), from passive ideation to attempts, had worse performance on an episodic memory task (NIHTB-CB Picture Sequence) than children without suicidality (Huber et al., 2022). This effect remained after adjusting for demographic variables, family history of suicide, and internalizing symptoms, suggesting a degree of specificity. No significant group differences emerged for fluid, crystallized, or total cognitive composites, indicating that observed effects were circumscribed rather than generalized across domains (Huber et al., 2022). Using penalized logistic regression in ABCD, Van Velzen et al. (2022) found that cognitive variables (NIHTB-CB, WISC-V Matrix Reasoning, Cash Choice Task [delay of gratification measure]) meaningfully contributed to classifying suicidal thoughts and behaviors, outperforming neuroimaging and genetic modalities but remaining less predictive than clinical psychiatric measures (CBCL, KSADS−5). In contrast, analysis of the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort found that post-pubertal youth with suicidal ideation demonstrated marginally better cognitive accuracy than peers without ideation, an effect absent in pre-pubertal youth and stronger among boys, suggesting that cognitive–suicidality associations may depend on pubertal status and sex, rather than reflecting a stable deficit profile (Barzilay et al., 2019). Together, baseline findings from the ABCD Study suggest that cognition plays a role in suicidality, with episodic memory, abstraction, and self-regulatory processes appearing particularly relevant.
Longitudinal
Longitudinal analyses suggested that baseline cognitive differences may prospectively predict first-onset suicidal thoughts and behaviors. Effect sizes appeared modest and operated within multivariate risk models rather than as stand-alone predictors. Extending prior ABCD modeling work by Van Velzen et al. (2022), Nguyen and colleagues (2025) demonstrated that biopsychosocial models created from baseline ABCD data successfully predicted first-onset suicidal thoughts and behaviors (KSADS−5) over four years of follow-up. Newly trained longitudinal models included lower baseline performance on matrix reasoning (WISC-V), reading (NIHTB-CB Oral Reading Recognition), and vocabulary (NIHTB-CB Picture Vocabulary) and predicted subsequent first-onset suicidal thoughts and behaviors relative to healthy controls and psychiatric controls without suicidality (Nguyen et al., 2025). Notably, reduced vocabulary and greater impulsivity differentiated youth who later made suicide attempts from those reporting ideation only (Nguyen et al., 2025), suggesting potential differentiation between ideation and attempt trajectories. Overall, longitudinal findings reinforced the idea that cognitive measures add predictive value to suicide risk models. However, effects are small and operate within broader biopsychosocial frameworks, underscoring that cognition likely functions as one component of suicide risk rather than a primary driver.

No studies were found.
Genetics

Psychotic-like experiences
Behavioral
Baseline
Weaker cognitive scores were associated with greater psychotic-like experiences (PLEs), with very small effect sizes (Table 4). Karcher et al., 2018, Karcher et al., 2020, Karcher et al., 2021 examined the relationship between PLEs and cognition using the child-report Prodromal Questionnaire-Brief Child Version (PQ-BC), other PLE-related items from the CBCL and KSADS−5, and family history of mental health diagnoses. Higher PQ-BC scores were associated with lower fluid and crystallized intelligence (NIHTB-CB) scores, with the strongest task-level associations observed for working memory, processing speed, and reading ability (Karcher et al., 2018, Karcher et al., 2020). Similarly, the NIHTB-CB total cognition score was negatively associated with total PLEs (Karcher et al., 2021). These findings corroborated previous research indicating that cognitive deficits—especially in reading, processing speed, and memory—can exist in childhood before the onset of psychosis (Calkins et al., 2014, Cannon et al., 2002, Niarchou et al., 2013, Niendam et al., 2003).
Longitudinal
Longitudinal findings suggest that cognitive functioning may differentiate the developmental course of PLEs in early adolescence. Youth with distressing PLEs (PQ-BC distress scores) that persisted between baseline and 2-year follow-up had worse baseline working memory, vocabulary, cognitive flexibility, fluid intelligence, and first cognitive principal component (derived from NIHTB-CB) scores, compared to peers with non-distressing PLEs (Karcher et al., 2022). Compared to children without PLEs at baseline, those with remitting PLEs had poorer working memory and verbal performance, while those with worsening PLEs had weaker working memory, suggesting cognitive deficits may accompany shifts in PLE status (Jia et al., 2024). Children with persistent, distressing PLEs across four years of follow-up exhibited steeper declines in cognitive performance across nearly all NIHTB-CB tasks, compared to children with low PLEs (Karcher et al., 2025). Youth with transient PLEs also demonstrated worsening verbal and memory trajectories compared to peers with low PLEs, suggesting that declining cognition may mark clinically significant PLEs (Karcher et al., 2025). Conversion to a “high-risk” psychosis state, as defined by elevated PQ-BC Distress scores at 3-year follow-up, was predicted by lower verbal memory scores (RAVLT) one year earlier, echoing prior longitudinal clinical high-risk (CHR) studies identifying verbal memory as a sensitive marker of emerging psychosis (Allott et al., 2019, Smucny et al., 2024). Finally, the association between baseline cognitive performance and the rate of worsening symptoms over time was substantially stronger in those with PLEs, and of large effect size (Table 4), compared to those with internalizing or externalizing symptoms, suggesting both a specific and greater effect of cognition on PLE trajectories (Karcher et al., 2023). Findings from the ABCD Study largely aligned with other work showing that youth at elevated psychosis risk display baseline deficits in memory, language, and executive functioning that shape symptom trajectories and later functioning (Bora and Murray, 2014, Carrión et al., 2013, Dickson et al., 2018).
Neuroimaging
Baseline
No studies were found.
Longitudinal
Several of the studies reviewed above also included neuroimaging, thereby drawing connections between neurobiology, cognition, and PLE trajectories. Jia and colleagues (2024) found worse cognitive performance in children with remitting PLEs between baseline and 2-year follow-up (see prior section); compared to those without PLEs, this group also showed later reduced cortical volume in the supramarginal gyrus and inferior temporal lobe, and reduced surface area in the supramarginal gyrus and lateral sulcus. Compared to the remitting group, those with increasing PLEs and weaker working memory displayed greater cortical surface area in the left temporal pole (Jia et al., 2024). Youth with distressing PLEs over a two-year period had weaker baseline cognition relative to peers without distressing PLEs, lower baseline total cortical and subcortical volumes, and decreased cingulo-opercular within-network and cingulo-opercular-cerebellum connectivity (Karcher et al., 2022, Karcher et al., 2023). Furthermore, youth with persistent distressing PLEs showed both worsening cognitive functioning and accelerated declines in global morphometry—including cortical volume, thickness, and surface area, subcortical volume, and intracranial volume—across baseline, 2-year, and 4-year follow-ups (Karcher et al., 2025). These results parallel prior neuroimaging studies in adolescent psychosis-spectrum disorders reporting progressive reductions in gray matter and cortical integrity (Arango et al., 2012, Bourque et al., 2017, Mennigen and Bearden, 2020, Rapoport et al., 1999, Satterthwaite et al., 2016; Thompson et al., 2001). Together, longitudinal ABCD findings consistently demonstrated that impairments in working memory, language, and executive functioning co-occur with widespread structural and functional brain abnormalities in youth with PLEs, providing evidence for the role of cognition–brain interactions in psychosis risk across development.
Genetics
Baseline
Karcher and colleagues (2022) found that total and distressing PLEs were associated with higher cross-disorder (anorexia nervosa, ADHD, autism spectrum disorder [ASD], BD, MDD, OCD, SZ, and Tourette syndrome) and lower educational attainment PGSs (Karcher et al., 2022). Lower educational attainment PGSs were also associated with lower intracranial, total cortical, and total subcortical volumes, which were in turn associated with higher PLEs (Karcher et al., 2022). Cognitive performance accounted for ∼32% of the association between educational attainment PGS and PLEs, and lower cognitive performance indirectly linked lower educational attainment PGSs to more PLEs (Karcher et al., 2022).
PRS scores of SZ in the baseline ABCD sample and the UK Biobank sample were negatively associated with fluid, crystallized, and total composite scores in boys, but not girls (Gui et al., 2022). In another ABCD study, Pedersen et al. (2023) applied Bayesian multilevel linear models and found that higher SZ PRSs were associated with lower task accuracy on the EN-back, though not with a g-factor (first principal component derived from the NIHTB-CB). In contrast, Pat et al. (2022a) found that SZ PGSs in the baseline sample were positively associated with higher p-factor, internalizing, externalizing, and neurodevelopmental symptom scores, but were not significantly related to cognitive ability. Furthermore, cognition did not mediate the relationship between SZ genetic liability and mental health symptoms (Pat et al., 2022a). Notably, prior work in a similar U.S.-based youth sample reported a positive association between SZ PRSs and IQ (Córdova-Palomera et al., 2018), though another study corroborated the negative association found in the ABCD analyses (Hubbard et al., 2016). Thus, within the baseline ABCD Study sample, genetics underlying psychosis spectrum disorders appeared to have links to general psychopathology, with some studies suggesting additional links to cognition.
Longitudinal
Karcher and colleagues (2025) examined the interplay between genetics, environmental risk (i.e., baseline neighborhood safety, estimated drug crime exposure, overall deprivation, poverty rate, and lead exposure risk), familial risk for psychosis, cognition, and PLE status across baseline, 2-year, and 4-year follow-ups. Greater environmental risk and family history of psychosis, but not SZ PGSs, were associated with membership in the persistent distressing PLE group compared to the low-level PLE group (Karcher et al., 2025). Park and colleagues (2024) found that lower educational attainment PGSs were associated with higher PLE scores at baseline and 1-year and 2-year follow-ups, whereas lower cognitive performance PGSs were linked to greater baseline distress (Park et al., 2024). Fluid and crystallized intelligence scores mediated the effects of cognitive PGSs on PLEs across timepoints, suggesting that cognitive PGSs may reflect both cognitive traits and shared genetic liability with PLEs (Park et al., 2024). Overall, cognition-related genetic liability may be a more sensitive marker of early psychosis risk than disorder-specific risk scores, highlighting the need for further longitudinal research to clarify mechanisms of risk transmission.
Substance use
Behavioral
Baseline
Given that most substance use does not emerge until the adolescent years, very few studies examined substance use with respect to cognitive tasks in the baseline sample of 9–10-year-olds. Zhang and colleagues (2020) found that greater caffeine consumption was linked to lower crystallized (vocabulary comprehension, reading decoding) and fluid (inhibitory control, working memory, cognitive flexibility, processing speed, episodic memory) cognitive domains. After adjusting for age, gender, sleep, and socioeconomic status, caffeine intake remained negatively associated with cognitive scores, except reading decoding and inhibitory control (Zhang et al., 2020). Results remained consistent after excluding children who had consumed caffeine within 24 h of testing, suggesting that associations were not driven by acute caffeine effects (Zhang et al., 2020).
Longitudinal
Longitudinal findings from the ABCD Study examined cognition as a risk factor for future substance use, as well as the effects of substance use on cognition. Jones and colleagues (2024) applied principal component analysis to baseline cognitive tasks (NIHTB-CB, RAVLT, LMT, and WISC-V) and found that higher scores on the mental rotation principal component were associated with lower odds of initiating tobacco use between ages 13–14, particularly in females (Jones et al., 2024). Higher scores on components reflecting general ability (for males and females) and learning and memory (for females only) were associated with greater odds of initiating alcohol use, while the executive functioning component was not significantly associated with either alcohol or tobacco use initiation (Jones et al., 2024). In contrast, Adams et al. (2024) found that baseline SST performance was not associated with positive or negative alcohol expectancies across the 1-, 2-, and 3-year follow-ups in the ABCD cohort.
Evidence linking substance use to cognitive performance was more limited. At the 4-year follow-up, Wade et al. (2024) found that greater cannabis exposure (measured via hair samples) was associated with lower scores on vocabulary and inhibitory control tasks (NIHTB-CB Picture Vocabulary and Flanker), while greater self-reported cannabis use correlated with poorer memory (Picture Sequence Memory and List Sorting Working Memory). Although cross-sectional, these results align with prior research linking substance use to deficits in memory, language, and executive function in adolescence (Ajmera et al., 2021; Clark et al., 2017; Khurana et al., 2017; Morin et al., 2019). Together, these early findings from the ABCD Study are consistent with other work demonstrating that cognition both influences and is influenced by substance use. Notably, the early findings that stronger cognitive skills may facilitate early experimentation with socially normative substances such as alcohol are somewhat at odds with prior work demonstrating the association between lower cognitive ability and future substance use (Khurana et al., 2017).

Longitudinal
Emerging longitudinal work in the ABCD cohort has begun to link cognitive performance and neural markers to substance use outcomes. During the EN-back working memory fMRI task at the 2-year follow-up, greater activation in the bilateral pallidum and right putamen predicted youth-reported alcohol sipping at the same timepoint (Delfel et al., 2024). Similarly, Assari and colleagues (2025) corroborated the importance of the basal ganglia, demonstrating that baseline hypoconnectivity between the left caudate and cingulo-opercular network correlated with greater future tobacco and marijuana use and lower baseline cognitive flexibility, processing speed, and fluid cognition scores. Structural equation modeling corroborated positive associations between baseline cognitive performance and corticostriatal functional connectivity, though the association between corticostriatal connectivity and future tobacco or marijuana use was not significant (Assari et al., 2025).
Using elastic net regression to predict tobacco and marijuana initiation at the 3-year follow-up, Green and colleagues (2024) identified reduced cortical thickness in the left anterior cingulate cortex and higher cognitive scores (NIHTB-CB Flanker, Picture Vocabulary, and Oral Reading; RAVLT learning) as significant predictors of initiation risk (Green et al., 2024). Although these factors significantly contributed to the predictive model, neuroimaging and cognitive measures did not substantially enhance the predictive accuracy compared to sociodemographic and self-report factors (Green et al., 2024). Together, these studies point to corticostriatal function, anterior cingulate morphology, and select cognitive domains as potential early markers of substance use risk, but larger and more targeted longitudinal analyses are needed to clarify how cognition and neurobiology jointly contribute to substance use trajectories.

Discussion of general findings
Currently available findings from the ABCD Study indicate that cognitive functioning is consistently associated with youth psychopathology, though the magnitude and direction of associations vary across domains. Multimodal analyses linking cognition to frontoparietal circuitry, white matter integrity, and polygenic risk provide converging evidence for shared neurodevelopmental pathways. For CBCL externalizing symptoms, ADHD, and disruptive behavior disorders, lower executive functioning, working memory, fluid cognition, and general cognitive ability were consistently associated with greater symptom severity. This relationship was robust to several analytic approaches, including those with latent and observable variables, predictive models, regressions, and correlations, and was supported by neuroimaging findings implicating frontoparietal and corticostriatal systems, white matter integrity, and neural efficiency. In ADHD specifically, polygenic risk scores were associated with both cognitive impairments and behavioral symptoms, with some evidence of cognition mediating the relationship between PRSs and behavioral phenotype.
In contrast, relationships between cognition and internalizing symptoms/disorders were heterogeneous. Internalizing symptom severity was negatively associated with executive functioning or fluid cognition, but positively associated with verbal and crystallized abilities. In some analyses, associations between internalizing symptoms and executive functioning attenuated after controlling for externalizing symptoms, highlighting the importance of examining comorbidity. Non-linear findings between cognition and symptom severity further suggested the need to model alternative relationship shapes, and for depression and anxiety, the identification of subtypes with differing relationships between cognition and symptom severity/diagnoses further illuminated this complexity.
Notably, studies on PLEs showed the most consistent and developmentally persistent cognitive associations, with somewhat larger effect sizes in longitudinal analysis. Worse performance in working memory, language, processing speed, and composite cognition differentiated youth with persistent or distressing PLE trajectories at baseline and across follow-up waves. These cognitive differences co-occurred with structural and functional brain alterations and were linked to cognition-related polygenic scores, particularly educational attainment PGSs. Compared to other symptom domains, PLEs demonstrated clearer longitudinal coupling between cognition and symptom progression.
Far fewer studies on suicidality, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, substance use, and autism spectrum disorder were found (Fig. 2). Suicidality was most consistently associated with episodic memory and language-based measures, but effect sizes were generally small and cognitive factors modestly contributed to predictive model performance. For obsessive-compulsive symptoms, cognitive differences attenuated after accounting for ADHD symptoms, suggesting that comorbidity may explain some of the observed impairments. Substance use findings indicated bidirectional and developmentally nuanced relationships, with baseline cognitive profiles predicting initiation patterns in some cases, and cannabis exposure associated with small declines in memory and executive functioning at follow-up.
Advances from the ABCD Study
The ABCD Study offers a qualitative advance over prior studies on cognition and youth mental health. By assessing cognitive functioning and multiple symptom dimensions within a large, population-based cohort recruited prior to the typical age of onset for most psychiatric disorders, the ABCD Study can directly address questions that single-diagnosis, clinical sample research cannot: whether cognitive vulnerabilities are transdiagnostic or disorder-specific; whether cognitive differences precede or follow symptoms; how comorbidity shapes disorder-specific profiles; and what features of these relationships are specific to late childhood and early adolescence. Many studies reviewed here tackled these questions, identifying new findings that both advanced our current understanding and opened doors to additional questions. Sophisticated methodologies were also at the forefront of the research. While many studies continued to use traditional case-control designs with linear modeling, a significant portion applied innovative approaches, with several themes emerging. Data-driven methods were particularly well-suited to the ABCD Study, given its large sample size and comprehensive data collection. Many studies applied dimension reduction approaches (i.e., PCA, CCA, latent factor analysis, etc.) to identify latent cognitive or mental health factors, often finding that a mathematically derived solution combining several variables reflected the appropriate variability needed to examine relationships between multimodal factors. Non-linear modeling allowed for more complex relationships between cognition and mental health to emerge, as seen in internalizing disorders. Similarly, a focus on parsing heterogeneity brought attention to the well-known interindividual variability in cognitive impairments that exists for those with mental health symptoms. Predictive models further cemented our understanding of the relationship between cognition and mental health, particularly when incorporating additional modes of data. These innovative approaches remain critical for fully understanding the complex relationship between cognition and mental health in youth.
A note on effect sizes
The small and very small effect sizes are worth addressing directly, as an immediate conclusion may be that cognition is not meaningfully related to youth mental health. Three points help put the effect sizes in context. First, effect sizes in population-based samples are expected to be smaller than in clinical samples. Clinical studies recruit participants based on symptom severity, which concentrates the sample at the extremes of the distribution and inflates effect size estimates relative to what one would find in the general population (Dick et al., 2021). The ABCD Study estimates associations across the full range of cognitive and symptom variability in a community sample, which is a more conservative test with resulting smaller effects.
Second, small effects often accompany multifactorial outcomes. Youth mental health is influenced by genetics, neurobiology, cognition, environment, and social factors—none of which individually explains the majority of variance. The same is true in psychiatric genetics, where individual variants explain small amounts of variance, yet genetics is widely considered critical to understanding mental health (Visscher et al., 2017). Rather, mechanisms of mental health, like genetics, are likely to be combinatorial, and assessing replication and mechanistic convergence is critical. For example, externalizing symptoms, ADHD, and PLEs meet these conditions.
Third, ABCD's large sample size affects the interpretation of small effects. With enough power to detect effects as small as r ≈ .05, a consistently replicated small association is unlikely to be a false positive. By contrast, larger effects in smaller clinical studies may partly reflect sampling variability and publication bias (Button et al., 2013). Small, stable effects in ABCD may be more trustworthy estimates of the true population relationship than larger effects from more selected samples.
Clinical relevance is crucial when interpreting effects of any size. Despite their small effect size in the ABCD Study, cognitive impairments have real-world consequences, including poorer functioning and educational outcomes. Furthermore, as cognition typically develops through adolescence into young adulthood, impairments during youth may have cumulative consequences as individuals pass through developmental stages. Thus, while cognition on its own may not be a primary driver of psychiatric outcomes in youth, considered alongside neural, genetic, and environmental data, it contributes meaningfully to characterizing children with psychiatric symptoms (Dick et al., 2021).
Proposed directions and conclusions
By integrating cognitive testing, clinical assessments, neuroimaging, genetic data, environmental measures, and repeated follow-up within a single large cohort, the ABCD Study enables questions that smaller or cross-sectional studies cannot address. Across domains, executive functioning, working memory, and language abilities were consistent correlates of psychopathology, replicating across analytic approaches in a population-based sample. Innovative questions and methodologies lent further nuance to these findings, as associations varied by symptom domain, attenuated when comorbidity was considered, or differed across cognitive subcomponents. Non-linear relationships and subgroup differences likely explain some variability, with patterns that only become detectable at ABCD’s scale.
The longitudinal and multimodal design of ABCD represents its greatest strength going forward. Baseline cross-sectional associations are well characterized, but prospective findings remain limited in several areas, including obsessive-compulsive symptoms, suicidality, substance use, and autism spectrum disorders. Forthcoming waves will enable clearer tests of directionality; specifically, whether cognitive differences precede symptom onset, track symptom progression, or reflect reciprocal processes. Ultimately, the value of the ABCD Study lies not in identifying single cognitive biomarkers, but in positioning cognition as one component within multimodal developmental models of risk and resilience.
